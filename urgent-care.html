Resuscitation

BLS: Adult & Pediatric

Source: AHA/AAP 2025 BLS Guidelines; ILCOR 2025 CoSTR

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Unresponsive + absent/abnormal breathing (gasping) + no definite pulse within 10 seconds
  • If uncertain whether a pulse is present — start CPR. Do not delay.
  • Witnessed sudden collapse — activate EMS and get the AED immediately, then start CPR
1

Recognize & activate (0–30 sec)

  • Check responsiveness, breathing, and pulse simultaneously — max 10 seconds.
  • Pulse check: carotid/femoral in adult/child; brachial in infant.
  • Call for help: request AED/defibrillator, oxygen, bag-mask, crash cart.
2

High-quality compressions

AdultChild (1y–puberty)Infant (<1y)
Depth5–6 cm≥⅓ AP depth, ~5 cm≥⅓ AP depth, ~4 cm
Hands2 hands, lower sternum1–2 hands1-hand heel or 2-thumb encircling
Rate100–120/min100–120/min100–120/min
Ratio (no airway)30:230:2 (1 rescuer), 15:2 (2)30:2 (1 rescuer), 15:2 (2)
3

Ventilation

  • Pulse present, not breathing normally: adult 1 breath/6 sec; child/infant 1 breath/2–3 sec.
  • Advanced airway: continuous compressions, ventilate without pausing.
  • Each breath over ~1 second with visible chest rise — avoid hyperventilation.
4

AED / defibrillator

  • Attach as soon as available — minimize hands-off time.
  • Children <8y: pediatric attenuator/pads if available, otherwise standard adult AED.
  • Clear patient → shock if advised → resume CPR immediately for 2 minutes.
  • Do NOT delay AED for IV access, medications, or intubation attempts.
5

Choking (FBAO)

SeverityAdult/ChildInfant
MildEncourage coughing, monitorAllow cough/cry, monitor
Severe5 back blows + 5 abdominal thrusts, cycle5 back blows + 5 chest thrusts (no abdominal thrusts)
UnresponsiveStart CPR, remove only visible object, no blind sweepsStart CPR, remove only visible object, no blind sweeps
6

ROSC & handover

  • Maintain airway, oxygenate/ventilate, monitor ECG/BP/SpO₂, treat hypoglycemia, avoid hypotension.
  • Handover: age, collapse time, witnessed/unwitnessed, initial rhythm/shocks, CPR start time, glucose, ROSC time.
Resuscitation

Anaphylaxis (All Ages)

Source: NIAID/FAAN (Sampson); WAO 2020; ASCIA 2024; AAAAI/ACAAI 2023

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Any airway compromise: stridor, throat tightness, tongue swelling, hoarse voice, drooling
  • Breathing: wheeze, persistent cough, dyspnea, hypoxia, severe asthma-like episode
  • Circulation: hypotension, collapse, syncope, shock, confusion, pale/floppy infant
  • Skin/GI + a systemic feature: hives/angioedema or severe abdominal pain/repetitive vomiting plus respiratory or circulatory symptoms
Interactive

Pediatric IM Epinephrine Dose Calculator

1 mg/mL (1:1000) IM into the mid-outer thigh · 0.01 mg/kg · max 0.5 mg

Enter a weight to calculate the dose.
1

Diagnostic criteria — ONE of three

  • Acute onset with skin/mucosal involvement AND respiratory compromise or reduced BP.
  • TWO or more of: skin/mucosal signs, respiratory compromise, reduced BP, persistent GI symptoms — after a likely allergen.
  • Reduced BP after a known allergen: adult SBP <90 or >30% drop; child — age-specific low SBP or >30% drop.
2

First 60 seconds — treat first

  • IM epinephrine into the mid-outer thigh immediately — repeat every 5 min if ABC problems persist.
  • Call EMS, state "anaphylaxis," request ALS, prepare AED/BLS.
  • Remove trigger, stop any infusion, lie flat with legs raised (left-lateral if pregnant/vomiting).
  • PHC/UCC rule: IM epinephrine has no absolute contraindication in anaphylaxis. Antihistamines, steroids, inhalers, or nebulizers must never delay it.
3

While waiting for EMS

  • High-flow oxygen if hypoxic/distressed/shock.
  • IV/IO access — do not delay epinephrine for this.
  • Normal saline bolus for hypotension/shock: adult 500–1000 mL rapid; child 20 mL/kg IV/IO, repeat as needed.
  • Monitor vitals, SpO₂, ECG; be ready for BLS/CPR and AED.
4

Adjuncts — supportive only, after epinephrine

MedicationAdult dosePediatric doseUse
Salbutamol neb5 mg, repeat/continuous<20 kg: 2.5 mg · ≥20 kg: 5 mgPersistent wheeze/bronchospasm after epi
Diphenhydramine25–50 mg PO/IM/IV1 mg/kg (max 50 mg)Itch/urticaria only
Hydrocortisone200 mg IV/IM4 mg/kg (max 200 mg)Not routine — refractory/delayed-onset reactions
Glucagon1–2 mg IV/IM, may repeat20–30 mcg/kg (max 1 mg)Refractory hypotension in beta-blocker patients
5

Refractory anaphylaxis

  • Ongoing ABC problems despite 2 appropriate IM epinephrine doses → urgent ALS/ED, usually IV epinephrine infusion (0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min titrated).
  • Prepare for airway failure: stridor, laryngeal swelling, exhaustion, cyanosis — notify ED early.
6

Disposition — never discharge from PHC/UCC

  • All patients treated for anaphylaxis are transferred to ED for observation (usually ≥4–6h; longer if severe/multiple epi doses).
  • Before ED discharge: epinephrine auto-injector prescription, written action plan, allergy referral.
Pediatric Emergency

Pediatric Status Epilepticus

Source: American Epilepsy Society; CHOP/RCH/Queensland pediatric pathways

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Continuous convulsive seizure >5 minutes, OR ≥2 seizures without return to baseline between
  • Any child not returning to neurological baseline
  • Seizure persisting after 2 appropriate benzodiazepine doses — activate EMS/ED regardless of further therapy
1

Rapid assessment (0–5 min)

  • Time the seizure and call for help early.
  • Lateral/recovery position, suction PRN, oxygen to keep SpO₂ ≥94%.
  • Do not place objects in the mouth or restrain.
  • Capillary glucose immediately — if low, D10W 5 mL/kg IV/IO.
  • Estimate weight, establish IV/IO, attach monitors.
2

First-line: benzodiazepines (5–20 min)

No IV/IO accessIV/IO access established
Midazolam IM 0.2 mg/kg (max 10 mg), mid-outer thighLorazepam IV 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg) over 2–5 min; may repeat once at 5–10 min
Midazolam intranasal/buccal 0.3 mg/kg (max 10 mg)Alt: Diazepam IV 0.15–0.2 mg/kg (max 10 mg); may repeat once
Diazepam rectal 0.3–0.5 mg/kg (max 20 mg) if midazolam unavailable

Maximum 2 benzodiazepine doses total, including any given pre-hospital. Activate EMS/transfer concurrently — do not delay for medication.

3

Second-line (20–40 min) — typically ED/PICU

DrugDose
Levetiracetam60 mg/kg IV (max 4,500 mg) over 5–15 min
Fosphenytoin20 mg PE/kg IV (max 1,500 mg PE) — cardiac monitoring
Valproic acid40 mg/kg IV (max 3,000 mg) — avoid in liver disease, caution <2y
Phenytoin20 mg/kg IV (max 1,500 mg), ≤1 mg/kg/min — cardiac monitoring
Phenobarbital20 mg/kg IV (max 1,000 mg) — risk of respiratory depression
4

Post-ictal & disposition

  • Monitor for respiratory depression after benzodiazepines; support ventilation if needed.
  • ALL children with status epilepticus require immediate ED transfer.
  • Look for cause: fever/sepsis/meningitis, trauma, toxins, electrolytes, missed anti-seizure medication, hypoglycemia.
Pediatric Emergency

Pediatric Febrile Convulsion

Source: Royal Children's Hospital; AAP; Queensland pediatric guideline

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Age <6 months or >5 years
  • Signs of sepsis/meningitis/encephalitis, non-blanching rash, focal deficit
  • Persistent altered mental state >1 hour, recurrent/prolonged seizure
  • Seizure >5 minutes → manage as status epilepticus
1

Definition & initial safety

  • Seizure with fever, age 6–60 months, no CNS infection/metabolic cause/prior afebrile seizure.
  • Time the seizure, protect from injury, lateral position, oxygen if needed. Do not restrain or put anything in mouth.
  • Check ABCs, SpO₂, temperature, glucose if ongoing/prolonged.
2

Classify

SimpleComplex
Generalized tonic-clonicFocal features or deficit
Duration <15 minDuration ≥15 min
Single seizure per illness/24hRecurrent within 24h/same illness
Full recovery <1 hourAltered mental state >1 hour

Prolonged/febrile status (>30 min or recurrent without recovery) — manage as status epilepticus and transfer.

3

Treatment

  • Active seizure >5 min: midazolam IN/buccal 0.3 mg/kg (max 10 mg) OR lorazepam IV/IO 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg). Max 2 doses total.
  • Treat fever source; antipyretic for comfort — does not prevent recurrence.
  • Simple febrile seizure: no routine bloods, EEG, imaging, or LP.
  • LP if meningitis/encephalitis suspected, or immunization incomplete/antibiotics may mask signs.
  • Anti-epileptic prophylaxis is not routinely recommended after a simple febrile seizure.
4

Disposition & parent advice

  • Discharge if simple, back to baseline, fever source managed, follow-up arranged.
  • Consult/transfer if complex, prolonged, respiratory/hemodynamic compromise, age <6 months, uncertain diagnosis.
  • Parent advice: lateral position, time it, no restraint/objects in mouth — call EMS if >5 min, breathing difficulty, blue color, injury, first seizure, or not waking.

Pediatric Gastroenteritis

Source: CDC & AAP Clinical Practice Guidelines

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Bilious (green) emesis, hematemesis, severe localized abdominal pain, guarding, or hematochezia
  • Bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, intractable vomiting, altered consciousness
  • Tachypnea (possible acidosis/pneumonia), toxic appearance, or suspected DKA (polyuria despite vomiting)
Interactive

Dehydration Severity Picker

Select the closest match for each sign — the overall estimate is a clinical gestalt, not a validated composite score

Estimated: Minimal / no dehydration (<3% loss)
1

Triage

  • Accurate naked weight — critical for fluid calculations.
  • Vitals: HR, RR, BP, temperature (≥38.5°C warrants closer evaluation for alternative infection).
  • Hydration history: wet diapers/24h, emesis/stool frequency, oral tolerance.
2

Dehydration severity

SignMinimal (<3%)Mild–Moderate (3–9%)Severe (>9%)
Mental statusAlertRestless, irritable, thirstyLethargic, unconscious
Heart rateNormalNormal–increasedTachycardia (late: brady)
Eyes/tearsNormalSlightly sunken/decreasedDeeply sunken/absent
Mucous membranesMoistSticky/dryParched/very dry
Cap refill/skin<2 sec, instant recoilProlonged, slow recoil>3–4 sec, tenting, mottled
3

Hydration management

  • Minimal: continue regular diet/breastfeeding; ORS 10 mL/kg per loose stool/emesis.
  • Mild–moderate: ORT trial in clinic — ORS 50–100 mL/kg over 3–4h, small frequent volumes (5 mL q1–2min).
  • Ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg (max 8 mg) single dose PO — for >6 months with persistent vomiting interfering with ORT.
  • Anti-diarrheals (e.g. loperamide) are contraindicated in pediatrics. Avoid routine antibiotics.
4

Disposition

  • Transfer via EMS: severe dehydration, shock, failed ORT trial, unreliable caregivers, or surgical/neurological red flags.
  • Discharge: well-hydrated, successful ORT, reliable caregivers.
  • Follow-up within 24–48h. Return precautions: no wet diaper in 8h, lethargy, intractable vomiting.
Pediatric Emergency

Pediatric Hypoglycemia

Source: ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines; ADA Standards of Care

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Altered mental status, seizures, coma, or extreme lethargy — unsafe to swallow
  • Glucose often <54 mg/dL with any severe symptom
  • Failure to fully regain consciousness, or inability to tolerate oral intake after correction
1

Severity

Mild–ModerateSevere
Glucose<70 mg/dL (<3.9 mmol/L)Often <54 mg/dL, or any level with severe symptoms
SymptomsTremor, pallor, sweating, hunger, tachycardia, irritabilitySeizures, coma, extreme lethargy
MentationAlert, safe to swallowAltered — unsafe to swallow
2

Conscious & able to swallow

  • Fast-acting carbohydrate 10–15 g: 120 mL juice/soda, 3–4 glucose tablets, or 1 tbsp sugar/corn syrup.
  • No honey in infants <1 year — botulism risk.
  • Recheck glucose in exactly 15 minutes; repeat dose if still <70 mg/dL.
3

Unconscious / seizing / unsafe to swallow

  • Recovery position — no oral fluids or food.
  • No IV access: Glucagon IM/SC — <25 kg (<12y): 0.5 mg · ≥25 kg (≥12y): 1.0 mg. Activate EMS.
  • IV access: D10W (preferred) 2–5 mL/kg IV bolus over 3–5 min, OR D25W 2 mL/kg. Avoid D50W in children (vein sclerosis, rebound).
  • Flush line with saline; recheck glucose after 5 minutes.
4

Post-correction & disposition

  • Once >70 mg/dL and fully alert: complex carb + protein snack to prevent recurrence.
  • Identify trigger: missed meal, unaccustomed exercise, insulin error, illness, accidental ingestion.
  • Discharge: known diabetic, clear correctable cause, recovered baseline, tolerating oral intake, reliable caregiver.
  • Transfer: unexplained hypoglycemia in non-diabetic, failure to regain consciousness, can't tolerate oral intake, oral hypoglycemic ingestion (needs 24–48h monitoring).
Pediatric Emergency

Pediatric Respiratory Distress — Bronchiolitis & Croup

Source: NICE NG9; RCH Bronchiolitis/Croup CPG; Queensland Paediatric EC; AAP Bronchiolitis

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Apnea, central cyanosis, exhaustion/listlessness, reduced respiratory effort
  • Poor air entry/silent chest, severe recession/grunting
  • Failure to maintain SpO₂ despite oxygen, or SpO₂ <90% (≥6wk) / <92% (<6wk or comorbidity)
  • Croup: stridor at rest with agitation/fatigue/lethargy/cyanosis, or silent chest, decreased LOC, apnea
Interactive

PRAM Score — Pediatric Respiratory Assessment Measure

Validated ages 2–17 for wheeze/asthma-type respiratory distress — use alongside clinical judgment for bronchiolitis, where it is less validated

Score: 0 / 12 — Mild
1

Rapid assessment

  • ABCDE + appearance: work of breathing, circulation, mental status, feed/speak/cry ability.
  • Position of comfort, keep with caregiver, minimal handling.
  • Oxygen if hypoxic/distressed; suction only if obstruction/secretions contributing.
2

Bronchiolitis severity (usually <2y)

MildModerateSevere / ED–EMS
Alert, mild/no recessionModerate recession, nasal flaring, RR often >60Apnea, central cyanosis
SpO₂ ≥92% in airPersistent SpO₂ 90–91% or <92%Grunting, marked recession, RR >70
Feeds >75% usualFeeds 50–75%, early dehydrationExhaustion, listless
No apnea/cyanosisYoung age/risk factorsFeeds <50%, dehydration, shock

Do NOT routinely use: salbutamol, nebulized adrenaline/steroids, antibiotics, CXR/bloods, or chest physio for typical bronchiolitis.

3

Bronchiolitis management

  • Minimal handling; oxygen if SpO₂ <90% (≥6wk) or <92% (<6wk/cardiopulmonary disease).
  • No routine suctioning — only if secretions cause distress/feeding difficulty/apnea.
  • Small frequent feeds/ORS if safe; stop oral feeds if severe distress or aspiration risk.
  • Lower threshold to transfer: age <3mo, prematurity <32wk, chronic lung/heart disease, feeding 50–75%, caregiver/access concerns.
4

Croup severity (usually 6mo–6y)

Barking cough, hoarse voiceStridor at rest, moderate recessionMarked stridor/recession OR poor air entry
No stridor at restAnxious but alert, good air entryAgitation, fatigue, cyanosis, hypoxia
No/mild recessionUsually normal SpO₂Silent chest, decreased LOC, apnea
5

Croup management

SeverityTreatment
MildDexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg PO once (or prednisolone 1 mg/kg). Discharge if stridor-free at rest.
ModerateDexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg PO once. Consider nebulized adrenaline if persistent/worsening stridor. Observe.
SevereActivate EMS. Nebulized adrenaline 1:1000, 5 mL undiluted. Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg PO/IM/IV (max 12 mg). Repeat adrenaline while arranging transfer.

Observe minimum 3 hours after nebulized adrenaline before considering discharge. Do not agitate the child or force a throat exam.

Pediatric Emergency

Febrile Infant (≤90 Days)

Source: AAP 2021; CPS 2024; NICE NG143; RCH Febrile Child

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Any infant ≤28 days with fever ≥38.0°C — always urgent ED transfer
  • Poor perfusion, shock, mottling, apnea, grunting, SpO₂ <92%, hypoglycemia
  • Lethargy, inconsolable irritability, seizures, bulging fontanelle, abnormal tone
  • Non-blanching rash/petechiae, neck stiffness, temperature instability <36.5°C
  • Any ill-appearing infant at any age
1

Assessment

  • Confirm fever — rectal preferred; don't dismiss reliable caregiver-reported fever.
  • Pediatric Assessment Triangle: work of breathing, circulation/perfusion, mental status.
  • Full exposure exam: fontanelle, skin/rash, joints, ears/throat, abdomen, hydration.
  • A viral URI, positive viral test, or recent vaccination reduces but does not eliminate serious bacterial infection risk.
2

Age-based risk & action

AgeRisk & action
0–7 daysNeonatal fever = emergency. EMS/ED now — full sepsis workup + empiric IV antibiotics usually required.
8–28 daysHighest invasive bacterial infection risk. ED/pediatric assessment — blood/urine/CSF, admission common.
29–60 daysStill urgent. ED risk stratification with urine, blood culture, inflammatory markers. LP/admission per pathway.
61–90 daysLower but not zero risk. Urine assessment at minimum; ED/peds if no source, red flag, abnormal vitals, or poor follow-up.

PHC/UCC rule: a febrile infant <3 months should not be discharged without senior pediatric/ED advice and a documented same-day plan.

3

While awaiting transfer

  • Check glucose immediately in any ill, lethargic, hypothermic, or seizing infant.
  • Don't delay ED transfer to collect urine; avoid bag specimens for culture decisions.
  • If septic/shock: isotonic crystalloid 10–20 mL/kg, reassess; don't delay life-saving antibiotics for cultures.
  • Antipyretics for comfort only — avoid ibuprofen <3 months.
4

Disposition

  • Most febrile infants ≤90 days require same-day ED/pediatric assessment.
  • Non-EMS private transport only if well-appearing, stable, no red flags, age >28d, ED agrees, short travel.
  • One-line handover: age (days), Tmax/method, appearance, vitals/glucose, red flags, transfer time.
Neuro / Vascular

Adult Status Epilepticus

Source: Neurocritical Care Society / American Epilepsy Society

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Continuous seizure >5 minutes, OR ≥2 seizures without full recovery between
  • Seizure persisting after first-line therapy — activate EMS concurrently, don't wait
  • Respiratory depression post-benzodiazepine — be ready to support ventilation
1

Rapid assessment (0–5 min)

  • Note exact seizure start time.
  • Maintain airway, oxygen to SpO₂ ≥94%, recovery position — do not place objects in mouth.
  • Capillary glucose immediately (treat if <70 mg/dL), IV access, continuous monitors.
2

First-line therapy (5–20 min)

No IV accessIV access established
Midazolam IM 10 mg (mid-outer thigh, vastus lateralis)Lorazepam IV 4 mg (rate 2 mg/min); may repeat once at 5–10 min
Diazepam rectal 0.2–0.5 mg/kg (max 20 mg) if IM unavailableAlt: Diazepam IV 10 mg (rate 5 mg/min)

Activate EMS and initiate transfer concurrently — do not delay for medication administration.

3

Second-line (20–40 min) — typically ED

DrugDose
Levetiracetam60 mg/kg IV (max 4,500 mg) over 15 min
Valproic acid40 mg/kg IV (max 3,000 mg) over 10 min — avoid in pregnancy/severe liver disease
Fosphenytoin20 mg PE/kg IV (max 1,500 mg PE) over 10 min — cardiac monitoring
Phenytoin20 mg/kg IV (max 1,500 mg), ≤50 mg/min — cardiac monitoring
Phenobarbital20 mg/kg IV (max 1,000 mg), 50–100 mg/min — respiratory depression risk
4

Disposition

  • All patients with status epilepticus require immediate ED transfer for neuroimaging and definitive care.
Adult Emergency

Adult Hypoglycemia

Source: American Diabetes Association Standards of Care

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Altered mental status, seizures, coma — unsafe to swallow (Level 3)
  • Failure to fully regain consciousness despite glucose correction
  • Recurrent hypoglycemia in clinic despite adequate oral intake, or no caregiver at home

Conscious & able to swallow — Rule of 15

  • 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate: 4 glucose tablets (preferred), 120 mL juice/regular soda, or 1 tbsp sugar/honey/corn syrup.
  • Avoid high-fat treats (chocolate, milk) — they delay absorption.
  • Recheck glucose in exactly 15 minutes; repeat 15g if still <70 mg/dL.
3

Unconscious / unsafe to swallow

  • Recovery position — no oral fluids or food.
  • No IV access: Glucagon 1 mg IM/SC stat. Activate EMS if no recovery within 15 min.
  • IV access: D50W 50 mL IV over 3–5 min (or D10W 250 mL). Flush line; recheck glucose at 5 min.
4

Disposition

  • Discharge: correctable cause identified, back to baseline, tolerating oral intake, competent caregiver present.
  • Sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia: prolonged observation 24–48h (long half-life).
  • Transfer: failure to regain consciousness, recurrent hypoglycemia despite intake, no caregiver available.
Adult Emergency

Hypertensive Crisis

Source: ACC/AHA 2017 Guideline

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • BP >180/120 mmHg WITH acute target organ damage → hypertensive EMERGENCY, activate EMS immediately
  • Neuro: altered mental status, severe headache + vomiting, visual disturbance, focal weakness, seizures
  • CV: chest pain, dyspnea, tearing back/abdominal pain, acute heart failure signs
  • Renal: oliguria, new hematuria, AKI  ·  Ophthalmic: papilledema, retinal hemorrhage
1

Confirm the BP

  • Quiet room, rest ≥5 minutes.
  • Re-check manually with appropriately sized cuff, bare arm at heart level; repeat in 10–15 min if still elevated.
2

Assess target organ damage (TOD)

  • 12-lead ECG — LVH, acute ischemia, arrhythmia.
  • Consider U&E, creatinine, urinalysis if resources permit.
3

Hypertensive EMERGENCY — BP >180/120 WITH TOD

  • Activate EMS immediately for ED transfer.
  • IV access if possible while waiting; oxygen only if SpO₂ <90%.
  • Do NOT attempt to rapidly lower BP with oral agents in clinic — can precipitate cerebral, coronary, or renal ischemia.
4

Severe asymptomatic HTN (urgency) — BP >180/120 WITHOUT TOD

  • Identify/treat triggers: pain, anxiety, urinary retention, medication non-adherence.
  • Target: gradual reduction ≤20–25% within the first few hours.
  • Restart home meds if missed; if starting new therapy use gradual oral agents (e.g. Captopril 25 mg, Amlodipine 5 mg).
  • Never use sublingual nifedipine — high risk of unpredictable BP drop → stroke/MI.
5

Disposition

  • Emergency: EMS transfer for IV antihypertensive therapy and continuous monitoring.
  • Urgency: discharge when asymptomatic, clear downward trend toward <180/110, triggers resolved.
  • Follow-up within 24–48 hours with strict return precautions.

Chest Pain

Source: 2021 AHA/ACC/ASE/CHEST/SAEM/SCCT/SCMR Guideline

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • STEMI, new LBBB, or obvious ischemic changes on ECG → activate EMS immediately
  • Any of the "Deadly Six": ACS, PE, aortic dissection, tamponade, pneumothorax, esophageal rupture
  • BP difference >20 mmHg between arms — think aortic dissection
  • Angina equivalents in women/elderly/diabetics: unexplained dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, fatigue, atypical-location pain

Immediate triage (0–2 min)

  • Bypass waiting room — immediate rooming.
  • Vitals in both arms; SpO₂.

The 10-minute ECG

  • Acquire and interpret 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of arrival.
  • STEMI/new LBBB/obvious ischemia → activate EMS immediately, don't delay for labs or history.

Adult Emergency

Acute Abdomen & Renal Colic

Source: Internal PHC protocol

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Signs of peritonitis: involuntary guarding, board-like abdomen
  • Pulsatile abdominal mass — suspected AAA
  • Severe pain disproportionate to exam findings — think ischemia
  • Signs of obstruction (vomiting, obstipation, distension), or fever with tenderness
1

Triage

  • Check HR, BP, RR, mental status — bypass to ED immediately if unstable.
  • Focused history: onset, migration, radiation to groin/testicle (colic), vomiting, fever, bowel/urinary change.
  • Exam: inspect for distension, auscultate, percuss for tympany, palpate for tenderness/guarding/rebound.
2

Renal colic — specific management

  • Presentation: sudden severe flank pain radiating to groin, nausea/vomiting, hematuria.
  • Diagnostics: dipstick urinalysis, U&E/creatinine.
  • NSAIDs are first-line for renal colic pain, absent contraindications.
  • Hydration: oral fluids if not vomiting; antiemetics + observe otherwise.
3

When to refer

  • Failure to control pain or persistent intractable vomiting.
  • Evidence of infection/urosepsis, single or obstructed kidney, suspected high-grade obstruction.
  • Pregnancy — requires obstetric/specialist consult.
  • Any red flag from triage above.
4

Discharge

  • Hemodynamically stable, pain controlled on oral meds, tolerating fluids, no sepsis signs, reliable follow-up.
  • Advise hydration, NSAIDs/analgesics, strain urine for stones.
  • Urgent outpatient/urology follow-up within 24–48h for imaging review.

Disposition

  • Adequate → discharge with 4-hourly bronchodilator plan, ICS/LABA, inhaler technique review, written action plan, follow-up.
  • Partial → continue steroids (oral 1mg/kg or IV hydrocortisone 200mg/day), observe for fatigue; escalate if no improvement by 4h.
  • Poor → continue bronchodilators/steroids, ICU consultation for possible admission.
  • Every exacerbation is a red flag — review maintenance therapy before the patient leaves. See the Adult Asthma chronic guide.
5

GINA 2026 divergences worth knowing

  • Oxygen: GINA 2026 revised thresholds down — supplemental O₂ not recommended unless SpO₂ <92%, and if given, the upper target is 95%. The SINA figures above are more liberal; decide locally which your service follows and document it.
  • SABA: GINA 2026 doses are more conservative than historically, citing over-treatment and SABA toxicity including lactic acidosis — which causes compensatory hyperventilation easily misread as worsening asthma, prompting still more SABA.
  • Reassess after the first dose rather than writing up multiple back-to-back doses unreviewed.
  • If anaphylaxis features coexist: give IM epinephrine first, then bronchodilators.
  • Shake the salbutamol inhaler before every actuation — otherwise a new inhaler can deliver ultra-high doses, a well-used one too little.
Neuro / Vascular

TIA & Stroke

Source: AHA/ASA 2026; NICE NG128; Canadian Stroke Best Practices; UK National Clinical Guideline

🚑 Refer to ER / Activate EMS now if
  • Any ONGOING focal neurological deficit → treat as STROKE until proven otherwise
  • Persistent deficit, decreased LOC, seizure at onset, thunderclap headache, anticoagulant use
  • TIA (fully resolved symptoms) is STILL a medical emergency — needs same-day assessment
1

Rapid recognition (0–5 min)

  • Time the onset — Last Known Well (LKW), ask family/witness.
  • Screen with FAST/BE-FAST: face, arm, speech, balance, eyes, time.
  • ABCs, lateral position if vomiting/reduced consciousness, oxygen only if SpO₂ <94%.
  • Capillary glucose immediately; consider mimics — hypo/hyperglycemia, post-ictal Todd's, migraine with aura, Bell's palsy, peripheral vertigo.
2

Persistent deficit or unclear recovery

  • Activate EMS/stroke code — immediate transfer to stroke-capable ED, never by private car.
  • Pre-alert ED: LKW/onset, deficits, glucose, BP, anticoagulant use, baseline function.
  • IV thrombolysis window: most time-sensitive within 4.5h from LKW; selected wake-up/4.5–9h patients may qualify after imaging. Thrombectomy up to 24h for selected LVO.
3

Completely resolved symptoms (suspected TIA)

  • Still urgent — same-day stroke/ED assessment, ideally within 24h.
  • No aspirin in PHC/UCC before imaging excludes hemorrhage.
  • Do not use ABCD2 score to delay referral.
4

While awaiting EMS

  • Keep NPO, support airway, lateral position if reduced consciousness/vomiting.
  • Do NOT rapidly lower BP unless hypertensive emergency/dissection/ACS — thrombolysis-candidate target <185/110.
  • No antithrombotics, no oral intake, no IM injections/sedatives before imaging.
  • Fluctuating or partially-recovering deficits can still be stroke — don't be falsely reassured.
5

Disposition

  • Transfer now: any persistent/recurrent deficit, reperfusion-window symptoms, severe headache/vomiting/decreased consciousness, seizure, anticoagulation, unstable vitals.
  • Never discharge undifferentiated focal neurological symptoms without a documented urgent stroke plan.
  • Document LKW/onset, BE-FAST findings, glucose/vitals, mimic features considered, last anticoagulant dose.
