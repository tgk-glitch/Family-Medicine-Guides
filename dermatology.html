Dermatology · primary care

The six that fill the clinic

Eczema, acne, tinea, urticaria, psoriasis, pigmentation. Between them these account for the large majority of skin presentations in general practice — and each has one classic trap that turns a simple consultation into a months-long problem.

Two things run through every section: how the condition actually looks in richly pigmented skin, because erythema-based descriptions fail here and the textbooks are still overwhelmingly light-skinned; and the regional traps — over-the-counter steroid-antifungal combinations, unregulated skin-lightening creams, black henna, camel contact, Hajj-season crowding.

About the images

Clinical photographs in published atlases and textbooks are licensed works — they can't be copied onto a site like this one, and reputable image banks like DFTB Skin Deep explicitly permit personal study only. So instead of two or three stock photos per condition, each section deep-links into the free atlases, where you'll find a full photo series and, on DermNet, a dedicated section on how the condition varies across skin types.

Each condition also has a slot for your own clinical photograph. Images from your own patients — with written consent, no identifying features — are the most valuable teaching material you can put here, because they show the presentations of the population you actually serve. The dashed frames mark where they go.

How much cream to prescribe

Under-prescribing is the commonest reason topical treatment "fails". One fingertip unit (a ribbon from the fingertip to the first crease, from a 5 mm nozzle) is about 0.5 g and covers two adult palm areas. Select the regions you're treating and this works out the total quantity.

Reference

Topical corticosteroid potency

Match potency to site and severity, then review. The commonest errors are a too-weak steroid used for months on a thick plaque, and a potent steroid used casually on the face or flexures. Brand availability varies — verify against what's actually stocked locally.

ClassPotencyExamplesWhere
ISuperpotentClobetasol propionate 0.05%; halobetasol 0.05%Thick plaques, palms/soles, lichenified skin. Short courses, specialist-level caution
II–IIIPotentBetamethasone dipropionate 0.05%; fluocinonide 0.05%; mometasone furoate 0.1% oint; betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointTrunk and limbs, plaque psoriasis, severe eczema flares
IV–VModerateTriamcinolone acetonide 0.1%; betamethasone valerate 0.1% cream; fluticasone propionate 0.05% cream; hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1%Body eczema, workhorse for most flares
VIMild–moderateDesonide 0.05%; alclometasone dipropionate 0.05%Face, flexures, children
VIIMildHydrocortisone 1% / 2.5%Face, eyelids, genitals, napkin area, infants
  • Vehicle changes potency. The same molecule is more potent as an ointment than a cream — ointments occlude and penetrate better. Ointments for dry, lichenified skin; creams for weeping or intertriginous areas; gels, foams and lotions for hair-bearing scalp.
  • Regional absorption varies enormously — roughly, eyelid and genital skin absorb many times more than forearm, and palms/soles far less. Site matters more than the number on the tube.
  • Do not use potent steroids on face, eyelids, flexures or genitals outside specialist direction — atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, steroid rosacea and perioral dermatitis follow, and withdrawal rebound then traps the patient.
  • Never apply a steroid to an undiagnosed scaly or annular rash — see the tinea section.
Interactive

UAS7 — urticaria activity score

The standard measure for chronic spontaneous urticaria. The patient records weals and itch once daily for seven days; each day scores 0–6, so the week totals 0–42. Enter daily scores to get the weekly total and activity band.

  • Weals (per day): 0 = none · 1 = mild (<20) · 2 = moderate (20–50) · 3 = intense (>50 or large confluent areas)
  • Itch (per day): 0 = none · 1 = mild · 2 = moderate · 3 = intense (interferes with sleep or daily activity)
  • Use it to judge whether escalating the antihistamine dose actually worked, rather than relying on recall at the next visit.

Eczema / Atopic Dermatitis

The itch that rashes — not the rash that itches

Your clinical photo slot

Flexural involvement in an infant or child — the single most recognisable pattern. Antecubital/popliteal fossae, or cheeks and extensors in infancy.

1

Recognise it

  • Itch is obligatory. No itch, reconsider the diagnosis entirely.
  • Age-dependent distribution: infants — cheeks, scalp, extensors, napkin area usually spared; children — flexures (antecubital, popliteal), neck, wrists, ankles; adults — hands, eyelids, flexures, often more localised and lichenified.
  • Morphology by phase: acute — ill-defined erythema, papulovesicles, weeping, excoriation; chronic — lichenification, thickened skin with accentuated skin markings, fissuring.
  • Background xerosis is near-universal — dry skin between flares is part of the disease, not a separate problem.
  • Supportive features: personal or family atopy (asthma, allergic rhinitis), early onset, relapsing course.
◐ In richly pigmented skin — read this before you look
  • Erythema is the least reliable sign. Inflammation appears violaceous, grey-brown, or simply darker rather than red — and may be nearly invisible under fluorescent clinic lighting. Palpate for warmth and induration; look tangentially in natural light.
  • Follicular and papular patterns are more common — small firm follicular papules on the trunk and extensors rather than confluent plaques. Easy to mistake for keratosis pilaris or a folliculitis.
  • Extensor rather than flexural involvement is well described and can throw you off the diagnosis.
  • Post-inflammatory pigment change is the outcome that matters most to the patient — both hyper- and hypopigmentation. Counsel early that the pigment change is the footprint of inflammation, not a scar and not a steroid side effect, and that controlling the eczema is what prevents more of it. This single conversation prevents a great deal of treatment abandonment.
  • Lichenification and prurigo-like nodules are more prominent.
  • Severity is systematically underestimated in darker skin precisely because erythema-weighted scores miss it. Score on itch, sleep disturbance, extent and lichenification instead.
2

The differential you must not miss

MimicWhat gives it away
ScabiesItch worse at night, whole household itching, burrows in web spaces/wrists, genital or nipple nodules. Any refractory “eczema” in a Hajj/Umrah season or crowded accommodation — examine for scabies before escalating steroids.
Tinea (steroid-modified)Annular with an advancing scaly edge and central clearing. Steroids flatten the edge and make it worse — see the tinea section.
Seborrhoeic dermatitisGreasy yellow scale, scalp/nasolabial/retroauricular, less itchy
Allergic contact dermatitisGeometric or unusual distribution matching an exposure — ask about black henna (PPD), hair dye, nickel, and skin-lightening creams
PsoriasisWell-demarcated edge, silvery scale, extensor rather than flexural, nail changes
Cutaneous T-cell lymphomaAdult-onset 'eczema' that never quite fits and never clears — biopsy
3

Treatment — the part that usually fails on quantity, not choice

  • Emollients are the backbone. Under-prescribing is the commonest error: an adult with widespread eczema needs 250–500 g per week, a child 150–250 g. Prescribe in 500 g tubs, not 30 g tubes. Apply within minutes of bathing, in the direction of hair growth.
  • Soap substitutes; short lukewarm showers. Avoid fragranced products.
  • Topical corticosteroid matched to site and severity — see the potency table and the fingertip-unit calculator above. Undertreating with a too-weak steroid for too long causes more harm than a short course of the correct potency.
  • Face, eyelids, flexures, genitals: mild potency only, or a topical calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) as a steroid-sparing agent — no atrophy risk, so suited to long-term facial use. Warn about transient stinging in the first few days or they will stop.
  • Proactive / 'weekend' therapy for frequent relapsers: after clearing, apply the steroid or TCI to the usual trouble sites twice weekly to prevent flares rather than chasing them.
  • Steroid phobia is the main adherence barrier and it is best met head-on: name it, explain potency and duration, show the fingertip unit, and give a written plan. A correctly used potent steroid for two weeks is safer than six months of an inadequate one.
  • Sedating antihistamine may help sleep during a severe flare; non-sedating antihistamines do not control eczema itch.
  • Bacterial superinfection (weeping, golden crust, pustules, sudden deterioration): treat S. aureus. Do not use topical antibiotics long-term.
🚩 Refer or escalate
  • Eczema herpeticum — same-day emergency. Rapidly worsening, painful, monomorphic punched-out erosions or vesicles, often clustered, plus fever and malaise. Needs systemic aciclovir. Missing this is the serious error in eczema care.
  • Erythroderma (>90% body surface involvement) — risk of fluid loss, sepsis, thermoregulatory failure.
  • Failure to respond to appropriate-potency treatment used correctly for 4–6 weeks.
  • Severe disease affecting sleep, school, or work — candidate for phototherapy or systemic/biologic therapy (dupilumab).
  • Suspected contact allergy requiring patch testing.
  • Recurrent infections, or diagnostic uncertainty in adult-onset disease.

Acne Vulgaris

Where antibiotic stewardship and scar prevention collide

Your clinical photo slot

Comedonal vs inflammatory vs nodular — three photos here would carry the whole grading section.

1

Grade it, because grade drives treatment

GradeFindingsStarting point
MildComedones predominate; few inflammatory papules/pustulesTopical retinoid ± benzoyl peroxide
ModerateNumerous papules and pustules; occasional nodule; truncal involvementTopical retinoid + BPO, add oral antibiotic if extensive or truncal
SevereNodules, cysts, sinus tracts, or any scarringRefer for isotretinoin — do not cycle antibiotics
Scarring or marked post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation at any grade upgrades urgency. Every month of delay in a scarring phenotype is permanent. Photograph at baseline with consent — it is the only reliable way to demonstrate response to a discouraged patient.
◐ In richly pigmented skin
  • Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is frequently the patient's actual complaint — they may present for the dark marks, not the spots. Treating the active acne is the treatment for the PIH; say so explicitly, and add rigorous photoprotection.
  • PIH can persist for many months and looks like scarring to the patient. Distinguish them: pigment change is flat; scarring is textural. Run a finger over it and show them.
  • Higher keloid and hypertrophic scar risk — a further reason to escalate early rather than temporise.
  • Pomade acne along the hairline and forehead from heavy hair oils and greases — ask directly.
  • Acne mechanica under sustained pressure or occlusion — relevant with hijab, niqab, headbands, and prolonged mask use. Not a reason to stop covering; manage with cleansing, non-comedogenic products, and treating the acne properly.
  • Counsel that harsh scrubbing and skin-lightening creams worsen both the acne and the pigmentation — see the melasma section on unregulated products.
2

Antibiotic stewardship — the rules that actually matter

  • Never use a topical or oral antibiotic as monotherapy. Always pair with benzoyl peroxide or a retinoid to suppress resistance.
  • Limit oral antibiotics to about 3 months, then stop and maintain on topicals. Cycling a patient through repeated antibiotic courses for years is both ineffective and a resistance problem.
  • Doxycycline or lymecycline are the usual choices. Avoid tetracyclines under 12 years and in pregnancy.
  • Maintenance is with a topical retinoid, not an antibiotic. This is where most relapse happens — patients stop everything once clear.
  • Counsel photosensitivity with doxycycline — a genuine issue at this latitude and in Hajj season.
3

Hormonal and specific situations

  • Female pattern acne (jawline, chin, premenstrual flare, resistant to standard therapy): combined oral contraceptive or spironolactone can be very effective.
  • Screen for hyperandrogenism if acne is accompanied by hirsutism, irregular cycles, or acanthosis nigricans — PCOS is common and often first suspected from the skin.
  • Pregnancy: retinoids (topical and oral) contraindicated. Azelaic acid and topical erythromycin are the usual safe options.
  • Isotretinoin requires strict pregnancy prevention, baseline and monitoring bloods, and counselling about mood — specialist-led in most settings.
🚩 Refer
  • Nodulocystic disease, sinus tracts, or scarring — isotretinoin candidates.
  • Failure after two adequate treatment courses (each ≥12 weeks, correctly used).
  • Severe psychological impact regardless of clinical grade — ask about mood, and treat this as a genuine indication.
  • Acne fulminans (abrupt severe ulcerating acne with fever and arthralgia) — urgent.
  • Suspected endocrinopathy, or sudden-onset severe acne in an adult suggesting drug cause or virilisation.

An untreated annular plaque with a raised scaly advancing edge next to a steroid-modified one. That contrast is the highest-yield teaching image in this whole guide.

1

Recognise the pattern by site

SitePatternNotes
Corporis (body)Annular, advancing raised scaly edge, central clearing, asymmetricThe classic. Scale lives at the edge — scrape there
Cruris (groin)Half-moon plaques on inner thighs, active edge, scrotum usually sparedScrotal involvement suggests candida instead

⚠ The trap that defines this topic — tinea incognito

A patient presents with an itchy rash. It gets a topical steroid. It improves for a few days, then spreads, loses its raised edge, and becomes an odd, poorly-defined, pustular or widespread eruption that no longer looks like anything. Now nobody can diagnose it.

  • Never prescribe a topical steroid for an undiagnosed annular or scaly rash.
  • Beware combination steroid-antifungal creams — widely available over the counter across the region, and a major driver of this presentation. Ask specifically what the patient has already applied, including anything bought without prescription or brought from abroad.
  • If a rash was steroid-treated and worsened, scrape it before doing anything else.
◐ In richly pigmented skin
  • The erythematous advancing edge may read as hyperpigmented or violaceous rather than red — the texture of the scaly edge and the annular configuration are far more reliable than colour. Run a finger across the border.
  • Extensive tinea corporis can present as widespread hyperpigmented plaques and be mistaken for psoriasis, eczema, or even cutaneous lymphoma.
  • Pityriasis versicolor is often the hypopigmented variant, and patients present distressed about white patches, fearing vitiligo. The fine surface scale and the sharp confetti-like border distinguish it; vitiligo is depigmented with no scale. Warn that repigmentation takes months after the fungus is cleared, or they will think treatment failed.
  • Post-inflammatory pigment change after clearance is common — again, not treatment failure.
2

Confirm the diagnosis

  • Skin scraping for KOH microscopy or culture — take scale from the active edge. This takes two minutes and prevents months of wrong treatment.
  • Confirmation is mandatory before oral therapy for nail or scalp disease, given treatment duration and drug interactions.
  • Consider zoonotic sources in the history — camel, sheep, and cattle contact (Trichophyton verrucosum) produces particularly inflammatory, often kerion-like lesions here.
3

Treatment

SituationTreatment
Limited corporis / cruris / pedisTopical terbinafine or an azole. Continue 1–2 weeks beyond clinical clearance
Extensive, or failed topicalOral terbinafine or itraconazole
Tinea capitisOral therapy always — topical alone never cures it. Griseofulvin or terbinafine per local protocol, with adjunctive antifungal shampoo to reduce shedding. Examine household contacts
OnychomycosisOral terbinafine, prolonged course — confirm first, check interactions and liver history
Pityriasis versicolorTopical ketoconazole shampoo applied to the trunk, or selenium sulfide; oral for extensive or recurrent

Reinfection prevention is the part that gets skipped: treat tinea pedis and onychomycosis if you are treating cruris (the feet are usually the reservoir), separate towels, dry the groin and web spaces, launder hot, avoid occlusive footwear, and treat family members and pets.
🚩 Refer
  • Any suspected tinea capitis where oral therapy or paediatric dosing is uncertain.
  • Extensive or recurrent disease in a possibly immunocompromised patient — including undiagnosed diabetes, which recurrent or florid fungal infection should always prompt you to check.
  • Diagnostic uncertainty after negative scrapings but ongoing suspicion.
  • Nail disease where the diagnosis or fitness for oral therapy is unclear.

Urticaria

Wheals that move — and the ones that don't

🔍 Visual atlas — opens in a new tab
DermNetPhoto series + a dedicated “Variation in skin types” sectionDermNet · In childrenRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDermNet · DermographismRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDermNet · AngioedemaRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDFTB Skin DeepPaediatric images across skin tones — search by diagnosis or body partDermNet A–ZFull index, if a direct link has moved
Your clinical photo slot

A well-formed weal with a pale centre and surrounding flare. Worth also capturing dermographism after stroking the skin.

1

Confirm it is actually urticaria

  • Individual weals last less than 24 hours and resolve without a trace. This single question does most of the diagnostic work — ask the patient to circle one lesion with a pen and check it the next day.
  • Intensely itchy, migratory, pale-centred weals with a surrounding flare.
  • Acute — under 6 weeks. Chronic — 6 weeks or more, subdivided into spontaneous and inducible (pressure, cold, heat, vibration, exercise, sunlight, water).
  • Angioedema accompanies urticaria in a substantial minority — deeper, non-pitting swelling of lips, eyelids, tongue, or genitals, often painful rather than itchy.
⚠ Two things that look like urticaria and aren't
  • Urticarial vasculitis: individual lesions persist beyond 24 hours, are painful or burning rather than itchy, and leave bruising or pigment behind. Needs bloods and biopsy, not more antihistamine.
  • Angioedema without any weals: think ACE-inhibitor–induced (may start months or years after starting the drug), hereditary or acquired C1-inhibitor deficiency. These do not respond to antihistamines or adrenaline in the usual way — a different pathway and a different emergency plan. Review the medication list every time.
◐ In richly pigmented skin
  • The surrounding flare is often not visible — you may see only the raised weal. Palpate; ask the patient to point rather than relying on what you can see.
  • Weals may appear as skin-coloured or slightly darker raised areas. Photographing in good natural light, or having the patient photograph at home during a flare, is genuinely useful since the eruption is often gone by the appointment.
  • Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation after resolution should prompt reconsideration of urticarial vasculitis, since true urticaria resolves without a mark.
2

Investigate sparingly

  • Chronic spontaneous urticaria needs almost no investigation. CBC and CRP/ESR is a reasonable baseline. Extensive allergy panels, food IgE screens, and imaging are not indicated and generate false trails.
  • Most chronic spontaneous urticaria has no identifiable external trigger — a substantial proportion is autoimmune. Saying this clearly and early is therapeutic; patients otherwise embark on exhausting elimination diets.
  • Targeted testing only where history points: thyroid function if autoimmune features, further workup if systemic symptoms, weight loss, fever, or arthralgia.
  • For inducible urticaria, provocation testing confirms the subtype.
  • Review drugs: NSAIDs and opiates can aggravate urticaria non-allergically; ACE inhibitors for angioedema.
3

Treatment ladder

StepTreatment
1Second-generation non-sedating H1 antihistamine, regular daily dosing — not as-needed. Standard licensed dose
2Increase up to fourfold the standard dose if inadequate after 2–4 weeks. Off-label but explicitly guideline-endorsed and generally well tolerated
3Add omalizumab — specialist
4Ciclosporin or other immunosuppression — specialist
  • Avoid first-generation sedating antihistamines for routine control — impaired cognition, driving risk, and poor sleep architecture.
  • Do not use maintenance oral corticosteroids. A short course (a few days) is reasonable for a severe exacerbation only; long-term use causes harm and rebound.
  • Set expectations: chronic spontaneous urticaria typically remits over months to a few years. Treatment suppresses, it does not cure — patients who understand this stop chasing triggers.

Psoriasis

A skin disease with a joint, a heart, and a mood

🔍 Visual atlas — opens in a new tab
DermNetPhoto series + a dedicated “Variation in skin types” sectionDermNet · ScalpRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDermNet · FlexuralRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDermNet · NailRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDermNet · GuttateRelated pattern worth comparing side by sideDFTB Skin DeepPaediatric images across skin tones — search by diagnosis or body partDermNet A–ZFull index, if a direct link has moved
Your clinical photo slot

A well-demarcated plaque with adherent silvery scale, plus a nail with pitting or onycholysis — nails are the diagnostic clincher people forget to photograph.

1

Recognise it

  • Sharply demarcated plaques with adherent silvery-white scale — the sharp border is the key contrast with eczema.
  • Extensor distribution: elbows, knees, sacrum, scalp, retroauricular. Symmetrical.
  • Look at the nails and the scalp in everyone — pitting, onycholysis, salmon patches, subungual hyperkeratosis. Nail involvement raises the likelihood of psoriatic arthritis.
  • Koebner phenomenon — plaques arising in scars, scratches, or sites of trauma.
  • Variants: guttate (abrupt small drop-like lesions after streptococcal throat infection, often in young people), flexural/inverse (shiny, minimal scale — easily mistaken for candida or tinea), pustular, erythrodermic.
◐ In richly pigmented skin
  • Plaques appear violaceous, grey, or dark brown rather than salmon-pink, and the scale often looks more prominent against the darker background.
  • Extent is greater at presentation on average, and the diagnosis is more often delayed — partly because erythema-based recognition fails and partly because eczema is assumed first.
  • Post-inflammatory pigment alteration after clearance is pronounced and prolonged, and patients often judge treatment as having failed when the plaque has actually resolved. Show them the texture has normalised even though the colour has not; set a months-long expectation.
  • Scalp involvement matters disproportionately with certain hair care practices — infrequent washing and heavy oils can complicate topical delivery. Ask how they wash their hair before prescribing a scalp regimen, or it will not be used.
2

Look beyond the skin — every visit

  • Psoriatic arthritis in up to a third. Ask about inflammatory joint pain (morning stiffness over 30 minutes, improves with movement), dactylitis (sausage digit), enthesitis (heel, plantar fascia), and low back pain. Joint damage is irreversible — early referral changes outcomes. Consider a structured screen such as PEST.
  • Cardiometabolic risk is elevated — screen for hypertension, dyslipidaemia, obesity and diabetes, particularly in moderate-to-severe disease. This is a genuine primary care responsibility, not an add-on.
  • Depression and anxiety are common and under-asked. Visible disease, especially on hands and face, carries real social cost.
  • Ask about smoking and alcohol — both worsen disease and matter for systemic therapy choices.
3

Treatment in primary care

SituationApproach
Limited plaque diseaseEmollients + potent topical corticosteroid with a vitamin D analogue (e.g. calcipotriol/betamethasone). Combination products improve adherence
ScalpPotent steroid in an appropriate vehicle (gel, foam, lotion); coal tar or salicylic acid to lift dense scale first, otherwise nothing penetrates

⚠ Do not give systemic corticosteroids for psoriasis

Oral or intramuscular steroids may appear to help briefly, then cause a severe rebound on withdrawal — including transformation to generalised pustular or erythrodermic psoriasis, which is a medical emergency. This is a well-recognised and avoidable iatrogenic disaster. It is worth saying out loud to any patient who asks for 'a steroid injection' because it worked for a relative.

🚩 Refer — and two that are emergencies
  • Erythrodermic psoriasis — near-total body erythema. Fluid loss, hypothermia, sepsis risk. Same-day admission.
  • Generalised pustular psoriasis — sheets of sterile pustules with fever and systemic upset. Same-day admission.
  • Suspected psoriatic arthritis — rheumatology, urgently. Do not wait for skin control.
  • Extensive disease, failure of appropriate topical therapy, or major functional/psychological impact.
  • Involvement of hands, feet, face, or genitals disproportionate to body surface area — these are functionally severe regardless of percentage.
  • Diagnostic uncertainty, especially flexural disease not responding to antifungals.

Your clinical photo slot

Centrofacial melasma in good, even natural light — pigmentation photographs badly under clinic fluorescents, so this one is worth taking properly.

1

Recognise melasma

  • Symmetrical, irregular, well-demarcated brown to grey-brown macules and patches on sun-exposed facial skin.
  • Patterns: centrofacial (forehead, cheeks, nose, upper lip, chin) — commonest; malar; mandibular. Extrafacial forms occur on forearms and neck.
  • Predominantly women; strongly associated with UV and visible light exposure, pregnancy, combined oral contraceptives, and hormonal therapy. Family history is common.
  • Wood's lamp helps gauge depth: epidermal pigment accentuates under the lamp and responds better to treatment; dermal pigment does not accentuate and responds poorly. Setting this expectation early avoids a frustrated patient.
◐ Skin phototype is the whole story here
  • Melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation are substantially more common and more persistent in phototypes IV–VI, and the psychosocial impact is frequently underestimated by clinicians who see it as cosmetic.
  • Visible light — not just UV — drives melasma in darker skin. This is why an ordinary transparent sunscreen underperforms: it blocks UV but not visible light. Tinted sunscreens containing iron oxides block visible light and outperform non-tinted formulations in melasma. This is one of the most useful, least-known practice points in this whole guide.
  • Any inflammatory dermatosis leaves more pigment behind — which is why controlling acne, eczema, or tinea promptly is pigmentation management.
  • Never treat aggressively without considering post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation risk. Over-vigorous peels, lasers, or irritating regimens in darker skin can cause exactly the pigmentation they were meant to treat, and it can be worse and longer-lasting than the original problem.
⚠ Unregulated skin-lightening products — ask every patient

Products marketed for skin lightening are widely used across the region, and a proportion of unregulated creams — bought informally, online, or brought from abroad — contain mercury, potent corticosteroids, or unlabelled hydroquinone at high concentration.

  • Mercury: nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and paradoxical pigmentation. The WHO has repeatedly flagged mercury in skin-lightening creams as a public health problem.
  • Undisclosed potent topical steroids: steroid-induced rosacea, perioral dermatitis, atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, steroid-modified tinea, and — with widespread long-term facial use — adrenal suppression. Withdrawal produces a severe rebound that drives patients back to the cream.
  • Prolonged high-concentration hydroquinone: exogenous ochronosis — a blue-black paradoxical pigmentation that is very difficult to reverse.
  • Ask non-judgementally and specifically: "what have you used on your face, including anything you bought yourself or that a relative gave you?" Patients frequently do not volunteer this, and they may not consider a cream to be a medicine. Check for SFDA registration where a product is named.
2

Treatment — foundation first, and it is not glamorous

  • Rigorous daily photoprotection is the treatment, not an adjunct. Nothing else works without it and relapse is otherwise guaranteed. Broad-spectrum SPF 50+, reapplied; tinted with iron oxides for visible-light protection; plus physical measures — shade, wide-brimmed hat, and the shading that headscarves already provide. UV index here is high year-round, and windows do not block visible light.
  • Remove the driver where possible: review combined oral contraceptives and hormonal therapy; melasma of pregnancy often improves postpartum, so avoid aggressive treatment during pregnancy.
  • Topical therapy: hydroquinone remains most effective; triple combination (hydroquinone + tretinoin + a corticosteroid) is more effective than single agents. Use for a defined, limited period with review — not indefinitely, given ochronosis risk. Non-hydroquinone options for maintenance or pregnancy: azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C, cysteamine, tranexamic acid topically.
  • Gentle everything. Irritation causes pigmentation. Stop scrubs, harsh cleansers, and layered actives.
  • Realistic expectations, stated at the first visit: improvement takes 3–6 months, recurrence is the norm on sun re-exposure, and dermal pigment may not clear. A patient who expects clearance in a month will move on to an unregulated cream.
3

Adding your own clinical photographs

The most valuable images on this site will be the ones from your own clinic

Every condition above has a dashed photo slot. Filling those with images from your own patients solves the problem the atlases can't: they show the presentations, skin tones, and product histories of the population in front of you. Practically:

1

Consent and de-identification

  • Written, specific consent for educational use on a publicly accessible website — general clinical photography consent usually does not cover this. Keep the signed form.
  • Crop to the lesion. No faces, tattoos, jewellery, distinctive scars, hospital wristbands, or name labels in frame.
  • Strip EXIF metadata before upload — phone photos embed GPS coordinates and device identifiers.
  • Follow your institution's and MOH policy; where a child is involved, guardian consent and extra caution on identifiability.
2

Taking a usable clinical photo

  • Even, natural light. Clinic fluorescents distort colour badly, and colour is exactly what you're trying to convey — this matters most for pigmentation and for erythema in darker skin.
  • Plain, uncluttered background. No flash directly on the lesion (specular glare hides scale and texture).
  • Two frames per lesion: one wider for distribution, one close for morphology. A disposable ruler or coin gives scale.
  • Same angle, distance and lighting on follow-up if you want a comparison to mean anything.
3
