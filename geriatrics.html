Part 00 · The Spine

Age-Friendly care & the 4Ms

Before any disease, this is the framework that holds the whole course together. Learn it first; everything else hangs off it.

The United States — and Saudi Arabia, and most of the world — is aging fast. As people age, care gets more complex, and health systems built for single-problem visits start to harm older adults: delirium, falls, adverse drug events, loss of function. The Age-Friendly Health Systems movement (IHI + The John A. Hartford Foundation) is the response. Its promise is simple: care that follows evidence-based practices, causes no harm, and aligns with what matters to the older adult.

The engine is the 4Ms: What Matters Medication Mentation Mobility. They are not four separate programs — they are one set, run together, on every older adult, in every setting, every time.

Analogy · the pre-flight checklist

A pilot doesn't skip the checklist because the weather looks fine, and doesn't run only the parts they like. The 4Ms are that checklist for older-adult care. A young patient can absorb a missed item; an older adult with low physiologic reserve cannot. Run all four, every flight.

Two verbs: Assess and Act On

Every M has two jobs. Assess = know the 4Ms for this person (ask, screen, review). Act on = build them into the plan and document so the next clinician across settings can see it. Knowing without acting is useless; acting without documenting breaks the chain.

High-Yield · the four questions that unlock the M's

If you remember nothing else, remember the order: What Matters comes first and steers the other three. A statin, a continence drug, or a hip surgery is only "right" if it serves the destination the patient actually wants. The 4Ms organize care around wellness and strengths, not just disease.

Trap · "we already do the 4Ms"

Most systems do some M's, some of the time, for some patients. Age-friendly means reliably — all four, all patients, all settings. The gap is rarely knowledge; it's consistency and documentation across hand-offs.

Going Further · the deeper architecture

The 4Ms are the practical face of two older ideas worth knowing for fellowship: homeostenosis (age-related narrowing of physiologic reserve — the real reason small insults cause big decompensations) and the geriatric syndromes (delirium, falls, frailty, incontinence, pressure injury — multifactorial states that don't fit one organ). The WHO's ICOPE model and the concept of intrinsic capacity push the same person-centered logic globally. The 4Ms are the bedside tool; these are the theory behind it.

Day 1 · Sunday

Assessment foundations

Normal aging vs disease · the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment · the 4Ms in practice · the screening toolbox with the numbers you'll be asked.

1.1 · Understand the patientNormal aging vs disease

The single most useful idea in geriatrics is reduced physiologic reserve (homeostenosis). With age, every organ system loses spare capacity. At rest, the older adult looks fine. Add a stressor — an infection, a new drug, surgery, dehydration — and the system that had no buffer tips into failure, and recovers slowly.

Analogy · the worn battery

A 25-year-old's organs are a new phone at 100% with fast-charging. An 85-year-old is at ~30% on a battery that's done thousands of cycles — perfectly usable at rest, but one demanding app (sepsis, an opioid, a fall) drains it fast, and it charges back slowly. Geriatric care is reserve management: protect the battery, and don't run apps you don't need.

High-Yield · atypical presentation is the rule, not the exception

Older adults present through their weakest link, not the diseased organ. Memorize these classics:

  • Infection (pneumonia, UTI) → delirium, falls, functional decline, anorexia — often without fever or leukocytosis.
  • MI → dyspnea, fatigue, confusion (silent MI), not chest pain.
  • Hyperthyroidism → "apathetic" (withdrawn, weight loss) rather than agitated.
  • Depression → somatic complaints & cognitive slowing ("pseudodementia").
  • Acute abdomen → blunted pain & guarding; deterioration outpaces exam findings.
Trap · "it's just old age"

Ageism is a diagnostic error. Weight loss, falls, incontinence, confusion, and fatigue are not normal aging — they are signals of treatable disease or geriatric syndromes. Equally, the opposite trap exists: aggressively "fixing" benign age-related numbers (mild forgetfulness, asymptomatic bacteriuria, an isolated lab) generates harm. The skill is telling the two apart.

Exam Q · predictable

An 82-year-old woman is brought in confused and unsteady for 2 days. Temp 37.0 °C, WBC normal. What is the single best next step?

Work up for an acute medical cause of delirium — start with infection (urinalysis/culture, CXR), review meds, check hydration/electrolytes. A normal temperature and WBC do not exclude infection in older adults. New confusion = delirium until proven otherwise; it is a medical emergency, not a psychiatric or "baseline dementia" label.
Going Further

Reserve loss is non-linear and organ-specific, which is why frailty (a measurable state of depleted reserve) predicts outcomes better than chronological age. Two older adults of the same age can sit at opposite ends of the fitness–frailty spectrum — the entire rationale for individualizing targets (DM, BP, lipids, cancer screening) rather than applying population guidelines by birth year.

1.2 · The structured lookComprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)

The CGA is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary evaluation of an older person's function, physical health, cognition/mental health, and social circumstances. It differs from a standard medical visit by adding non-medical domains, centering function and quality of life over disease counts, and (ideally) using a team — physician, nurse, pharmacist, PT/OT, dietitian, social worker.

Analogy · the full vehicle inspection

A normal visit checks the engine — the chief complaint. The CGA is the multi-point inspection: engine, brakes, tyres, lights — and whether the driver can still safely drive and has somewhere to park at night. It catches the failures that the engine light never shows: the slow gait, the missed medications, the empty fridge, the lonely apartment.

High-Yield · function is the vital sign of geriatrics

The hierarchy of loss runs IADL → ADL. Complex tasks (managing money, medications) fail before basic ones (feeding, toileting). A new IADL deficit is an early warning — often the first visible sign of dementia, depression, or a new illness — long before an ADL is lost. You can read function for free by watching the patient: unbuttoning a shirt, rising from a chair, climbing onto the table.

Trap · the busy-clinic problem

A full CGA is too long for one 15-minute visit. The fix is the "rolling" assessment: target one domain per visit and build the picture over time, plus patient-completed questionnaires before the appointment. Skipping it entirely because "there's no time" is how the falls, the missed dementia, and the unsafe home get missed.

Exam Q · Katz vs Lawton

A patient can no longer manage her own medications or finances but still bathes, dresses, and feeds herself. Which is impaired — ADL or IADL — and what does it suggest?

IADL impairment with preserved ADL. Medication and money management are IADLs. Isolated IADL loss is an early, sensitive marker — prompt cognitive and mood screening and a safety review; it frequently heralds early dementia.

The CGA's output is a unified problem list — medical, functional, nutritional, and social problems with proposed interventions — that the whole interdisciplinary team works from. That problem list is the deliverable.

1.3 · From framework to bedsideThe 4Ms in practice

Part 00 gave you the spine. Here is how each M actually runs — and how it shifts between the ward and the clinic, which is a favourite exam and OSCE point.

What Matters — the compass

Ask open questions and document specifics: "What matters most to you?" · "What would you most like to be able to do?" · for serious illness, "What are your most important goals if your health worsens?" Beware generic answers copied across patients — that signals a shallow conversation. Ask people with dementia too; ask people with delirium when they're least symptomatic.

Medication — the audit

Review everything (prescription, OTC, herbal). The IHI "look-hard" list: benzodiazepines, opioids, strongly anticholinergic drugs (e.g., diphenhydramine), all sedative/sleep agents, muscle relaxants, TCAs/antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers. Target meds with no indication, no response, no longer needed, duplicated, or not being taken. Tools: Beers + STOPP/START (Day 3).

Mentation — the three brain checks

Delirium, dementia, depression — the 3 Ds (full comparison in Day 3). In hospital, delirium dominates: screen at least every 12 hours and with any change in function/behaviour (UB-CAM/CAM). In clinic, the emphasis is dementia (Mini-Cog) and depression (PHQ-2). There is no approved drug to treat delirium — prevention and cause-hunting are the whole game.

Mobility — keep them moving safely

Screen fall risk (TUG). In hospital: mobilize ≥3×/day, out of bed for meals, PT early, and aggressively avoid restraints and remove catheters/tethers (they're chemical/physical anchors that breed delirium, deconditioning, and pressure injury). In clinic: educate safe mobility, fix what limits it, ensure a safe home, set a daily mobility goal.

High-Yield · the one-line difference

Delirium screening is mandatory in the hospital, not in the ambulatory setting. The clinic's Mentation focus shifts to dementia + depression screening. If a question contrasts settings, that's almost always the answer.

Going Further · the evidence base for each M

Each M rests on real trials worth a citation in a teaching session: delirium prevention via the multicomponent HELP programme (Inouye); early mobility reducing hospital-associated disability; deprescribing trials reducing falls and ADEs; and "What Matters" operationalized through Patient Priorities Care and the Serious Illness Conversation Guide. The 4Ms package these into something a whole system can run reliably.

High-Yield · Mini-Cog scoring (a perennial question)

3-word recall (0–3) + clock-draw (normal/abnormal):

  • Recall 0 → positive screen (clock irrelevant).
  • Recall 3 → negative screen (clock irrelevant).
  • Recall 1–2 → the clock decides: normal clock = negative, abnormal clock = positive.

The clock asked for is "ten minutes after eleven o'clock (11:10)" — a known trap that tests whether the patient maps "10" to the 2, not the 10.

High-Yield · CAM = the delirium algorithm

Delirium is present when you have (1) acute onset & fluctuating course AND (2) inattention, PLUS either (3) disorganized thinking OR (4) altered level of consciousness. In shorthand: 1 + 2 + (3 or 4). Inattention is the cardinal, can't-skip feature.

Trap · screening ≠ diagnosing

A positive Mini-Cog or PHQ-2 is a flag, not a diagnosis — it triggers a full work-up/referral, not a prescription. And the tools only work if used correctly and re-trained: in the IHI guide, an implausibly low delirium rate after rollout usually means the screen is being done wrong, not that delirium vanished.

Exam Q · pick the tool

For routine cognitive screening in a busy family-medicine clinic — including patients with limited English — which instrument is preferred and why?

Mini-Cog. It is fast (~3 min), simple, accurate, and largely language-independent (3-word recall + clock-draw), making it the AAFP-preferred primary-care cognitive screen. Reserve MoCA/SLUMS for fuller characterization after a positive screen.

Day 2 · Monday

Tailoring chronic disease

The same diseases, re-engineered for reduced reserve — diabetes, hypertension, IHD, and lipids — plus the 4Ms made operational, physiotherapy's role in falls, nutrition/sarcopenia in depth, and the conversations that carry the plan home.

2.1 · The flagship topicDiabetes in older adults — in depth

Diabetes affects over 29% of adults over 65 — and in this group it behaves like a different disease. The governing principle inverts what you learned for younger adults: hypoglycemia, not hyperglycemia, is usually the bigger threat. Tight control buys benefit only after years (a horizon many older patients don't have), while a single severe hypoglycemic episode can cause a fall, a fracture, an arrhythmia, an admission, or accelerate cognitive decline — and hypoglycemia itself impairs the ability to recognize and treat the next hypoglycemic episode, a vicious loop.

Analogy · sliding-scale = braking after the crash

Sliding-scale insulin chases glucose after it has already spiked — like only braking once you've hit the car ahead. Basal insulin anticipates the curve. That's why modern guidance moves people off reactive sliding scale and onto a planned, simplified basal (± agent) regimen.

High-Yield · annual screening is now an explicit standard

ADA 13.2: screen at least annually for the geriatric syndromes that diabetes both causes and is worsened by — cognitive impairment, depression, urinary incontinence, falls, persistent pain, and frailty — plus hypoglycemia and polypharmacy. This is a named recommendation, not a vague suggestion, and it is exactly the kind of "what should you screen for annually" question this material generates.

Cognitive screening deserves its own emphasis here: cognitive impairment makes carb-counting, insulin-dose calculation, and hypoglycemia recognition all harder — which increases hypoglycemia risk — which then worsens cognition further. Annual screening (Mini-Cog/MoCA/MMSE) is indicated for all diabetics ≥65, and especially when self-care suddenly falters: insulin-dose errors, missed meals, missed doses, or new difficulty recognizing hypoglycemia.

High-Yield · the pattern that makes it stick

As health worsens, every number relaxes upward: A1C 7→8→none, fasting floor rises 80→90→100, BP 130→130→140. The only thing that gets tighter is your vigilance about hypoglycemia and treatment burden.

Going Further · when A1C itself lies to you

A1C becomes unreliable in conditions common in older adults that alter red-cell turnover: end-stage kidney disease, recent significant blood loss, and erythropoietin therapy all distort it. In these patients, lean on blood glucose monitoring and/or CGM rather than A1C for goal-setting; fructosamine is a secondary option. There is also a validated tool worth knowing for the fellowship track — the LEAD (Life Expectancy for Older Adults with Diabetes) calculator — which helps anchor "is tight control worth it for this person" in data rather than gestalt.

Step 3 — modern monitoring: CGM in older adults

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has moved from "type 1 only" to a genuine geriatric tool. It directly serves the Mentation and Medication Ms: it reduces hypoglycemia, reduces fingersticks (a real burden for arthritic hands and impaired vision), and gives caregivers visibility.

  • Recommended for older adults with type 1 diabetes (reduces A1C, hypoglycemia, and treatment burden).
  • Offer for older adults with type 2 diabetes on insulin — RCT evidence (the WISDM trial in adults with T1D using MDI; real-world Medicare data showing ~10% TIR improvement with hybrid closed-loop) increasingly extends to T2D on insulin too.
  • Targets when CGM is used: healthy/complex → time-in-range (70–180 mg/dL) >70%, time-below-range (<70) <1–4%. Very complex/poor health → relax to TIR >50%, TBR <1%, with hyperglycemia tolerated more than hypoglycemia.
  • Reassess periodically — device burden (insertion, alarms, technology literacy) can outweigh benefit as cognitive or functional status declines; CGM is a tool to revisit, not a permanent fixture.

Trap · the antibiotic that triggers a hypo

A patient stable on glipizide for years is started on co-trimoxazole or a fluoroquinolone for a UTI — and lands hypoglycemic. Both drug classes potentiate sulfonylureas. Reduce or hold the sulfonylurea for the duration of the antibiotic course, and warn the patient.

Step 5 — simplify before you intensify

"Deintensification" and "simplification" are different actions — know the distinction: simplification reduces the complexity of a regimen (fewer injections, fewer fingersticks, no more sliding-scale math) without necessarily changing control; deintensification/deprescribing reduces the dose or stops a drug because the harms now outweigh the benefit. Both are endorsed, evidence-based, and safe — this is no longer a fringe idea.

High-Yield · the actual insulin-simplification algorithm (ADA Fig. 13.2)
  1. Change basal insulin timing from bedtime → morning.
  2. Titrate basal to a fasting goal of 90–150 mg/dL (adjust by ±2 units/week based on finger-sticks).
  3. If on prandial insulin >10 units/dose: cut the dose 50% and add a non-insulin agent; keep titrating prandial down as the new agent titrates up, aiming to discontinue prandial insulin entirely.
  4. If prandial insulin is ≤10 units/dose: just stop it and add a non-insulin agent (metformin if eGFR ≥45; otherwise a second-line agent).
  5. If on premixed insulin: switch to basal-only using ~70% of the total prior dose, given each morning.
  6. Never give rapid/short-acting insulin at bedtime. A simplified sliding scale can bridge the transition (e.g., 2 units for glucose >250, 4 units for >350) but should be stopped once not needed daily — it's a bridge, not a destination.

Step 6 — long-term care & end of life: the three-tier framework

High-Yield · the palliative-care diabetes recommendation, verbatim logic

When palliative care is needed: strict glucose and BP control are not necessary; medication plans can be simplified; lipid-lowering intensity can be relaxed, and withdrawal of statins may be appropriate (a multicenter trial found statin withdrawal in palliative diabetes patients improved quality of life). The goal becomes comfort, dignity, and avoiding both hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia/dehydration — not a number on a lab slip.

Exam Q · the three-tier framework in action

A 90-year-old with type 2 diabetes is enrolled in hospice with days-to-weeks prognosis and is taking little by mouth. Her family asks whether to continue her metformin and glipizide.

Discontinue both. She fits the "dying" tier — with minimal oral intake, continuing oral agents (especially the hypoglycemia-prone glipizide) offers no benefit and real risk. If she develops symptomatic hyperglycemia, a small dose of basal insulin can be added for comfort, but routine glycemic agents should stop.
Trap · the overtreated diabetic

An A1C of 6.2% in an 84-year-old on a sulfonylurea or insulin is not a triumph — it's overtreatment and a hypoglycemia time-bomb. The ADA explicitly lists overtreatment as a harm to avoid. "Good control" on the chart can mean the target itself is wrong for the person.

2.2 · Pressure, carefullyHypertension in older adults — in depth

The pathophysiology that changes everything

Younger hypertension is usually a problem of vascular resistance; older hypertension is usually a problem of vascular stiffness. Years of arterial wall changes (collagen deposition, elastin fragmentation) raise systolic pressure while diastolic pressure often falls — producing the classic geriatric pattern: isolated systolic hypertension with a widened pulse pressure. That same stiff system has blunted baroreceptor reflexes, which is precisely why these patients are so prone to orthostatic hypotension when you treat the systolic number.

Analogy · the rigid garden hose

A young, supple hose absorbs a pressure surge by flexing. An old, calcified hose can't flex — the pressure spike has nowhere to go but straight to the far end (your brain, your kidneys). That's isolated systolic hypertension: the pipe lost its give. But the same rigid pipe also can't compensate quickly when you stand up and pressure needs to redistribute — hence orthostasis.

Before you treat: rule out the measurement traps

  • White-coat hypertension (high in clinic, normal at home) and masked hypertension (normal in clinic, high at home) both become more common with age. If clinic readings don't match the clinical picture, get home or ambulatory BP monitoring before intensifying therapy.
  • Pseudohypertension — severely calcified, non-compressible brachial arteries can give a falsely high cuff reading. Suspect it when BP looks high but the patient has no end-organ damage and develops symptoms of hypotension at "normal" doses.
  • Orthostatic measurement, done properly: BP and pulse supine after 5 minutes rest, then again at 1 and 3 minutes standing. A drop of ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic (with or without symptoms) defines orthostatic hypotension. Do this routinely in anyone on antihypertensives, not just those who report dizziness.
High-Yield · the J-curve

Below a certain SBP (roughly <120–130 in frail elders, lower in those with coronary disease), further lowering may increase cardiovascular events and falls — coronary perfusion in a stiff, atherosclerotic vessel depends on a minimum diastolic pressure. This is why "lower is always better" is false in this population, and why a standing BP, not just a seated one, should guide titration.

Exam Q · pick the next move

An 81-year-old on amlodipine and lisinopril has a clinic BP of 152/86 but reports two episodes of near-syncope on standing this week. Best next step?

Measure orthostatic BP before changing anything. Near-syncope on standing suggests orthostatic hypotension, which intensifying therapy would worsen. If orthostatic drop is confirmed, consider deintensifying rather than adding a third agent for the clinic number alone.

2.3 · The aging heartIHD, heart failure & atrial fibrillation — in depth

Ischemic heart disease: secondary vs primary prevention is the whole story

For established ASCVD (secondary prevention), evidence is strong and age-blind: START recommends a statin (unless end-of-life or established moderate–severe frailty), an ACE inhibitor for CAD, and a β-blocker for symptomatic CAD. This is the inverse of primary prevention (§2.4) — here the error to avoid is under-treatment, not over-treatment.

Stable angina: first-line is a β-blocker or CCB; add a long-acting nitrate if symptoms persist. Revascularization decisions (PCI/CABG) in older adults should weigh frailty and functional status alongside anatomy — a CGA-informed decision, not anatomy alone, since periprocedural delirium, bleeding, and contrast nephropathy risk all rise sharply with frailty. A formal frailty assessment (gait speed, grip strength, CFS) before elective revascularization is increasingly standard practice and a good fellowship-level talking point.

Heart failure — full GDMT, with the geriatric caveats layered on

HFrEF gets the complete four-pillar regimen: ACEI/ARB or sacubitril–valsartan, β-blocker, MRA, and an SGLT2 inhibitor — now indicated across the EF spectrum (HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF), with or without diabetes. Don't withhold GDMT purely on the basis of age; titrate slower and monitor more closely instead.

  • Diuretics: for congestion, not for blood pressure — use the lowest effective loop-diuretic dose; over-diuresis causes the orthostatic falls and acute kidney injury that land frail HF patients back in hospital.
  • Iron deficiency (even without anemia) is common in older HF patients and worsens symptoms/exercise capacity — check ferritin/TSAT and consider IV iron repletion, which improves quality of life independent of EF.
  • Avoid non-dihydropyridine CCBs (diltiazem, verapamil) and NSAIDs in HFrEF — both can precipitate decompensation.
  • Digoxin is not first-line; if used, cap at ≤0.125 mg/day and avoid at that dose if eGFR <30 — narrow therapeutic window, classic Beers PIM.
  • Device therapy (ICD, CRT) decisions in advanced age should explicitly weigh remaining life expectancy and goals — an ICD that prevents sudden death but not heart-failure progression may not serve a patient whose priority is avoiding hospitalizations near the end of life.

Atrial fibrillation — rate, rhythm, and anticoagulation

  • Anticoagulate per CHA₂DS₂-VASc; DOACs are preferred over warfarin for most (fewer intracranial bleeds, no INR monitoring, fewer interactions). Apixaban is generally preferred for long-term AF/VTE; warfarin remains first-line for mechanical valves, moderate–severe mitral stenosis, or eGFR <15.
  • Rate control is the default strategy for most older adults: β-blocker or a non-DHP CCB (only with preserved EF). Amiodarone is reserved for those with heart failure or significant LVH — its long half-life and multi-organ toxicity (thyroid, lung, liver) make it a poor general-purpose choice in frail elders.
  • Rhythm control (cardioversion, ablation) is reasonable in symptomatic patients with good functional status; age alone is not a contraindication to ablation, but procedural risk and recurrence rates should be discussed explicitly.
Trap · withholding anticoagulation "because they might fall"

The classic error. A patient would need to fall roughly 295 times in one year for the bleeding risk to outweigh the stroke-prevention benefit of anticoagulation. Fall risk alone is not a reason to withhold a DOAC — modify the fall risk instead (deprescribe sedatives, strength/balance training, home safety).

Exam Q · choose the anticoagulant

A 79-year-old with new non-valvular AF, CHA₂DS₂-VASc of 4, eGFR 55, and a history of two mechanical falls. Best anticoagulation choice?

Start a DOAC — apixaban is preferred. The fall history is not a contraindication; address the falls instead. Warfarin would only be first-line for a mechanical valve, moderate–severe mitral stenosis, or eGFR <15.
Going Further · SPRINT vs the patient in front of you

SPRINT (and its ≥75 subgroup) showed intensive BP control (SBP <120) reduced events even in fit older adults — but enrolled community-dwelling, non-frail people, excluded nursing-home residents, dementia, and those with orthostatic hypotension, and used unattended automated BP (reads ~5–10 mmHg lower than a standard clinic cuff). Don't extrapolate SPRINT to the frail patient it never studied.

2.4 · Shared-decision territory, with the evidenceHypercholesterolemia after 75 — in depth

Split the question in two — it changes the whole answer:

  • Secondary prevention (established ASCVD): continue the statin. Benefit is clear and age-independent; don't stop unless end-of-life or severe frailty.
  • Primary prevention (no ASCVD): this is where the 2025 AGS/NLA consensus lives — "it depends, decide together."
Analogy · planting an oak tree

A statin for primary prevention is an oak sapling — planted for shade years from now. If you'll be around to sit under it, plant it. If your remaining time is shorter than the tree needs to grow, you do all the digging and watering (pills, side effects, cost) and never get the shade. That is the time-to-benefit vs life-expectancy calculus at the heart of geriatric prevention.

The actual trial evidence in adults ≥75 (primary prevention)

Only ~8% of participants across 28 major statin trials were over 75, and only 2% of primary-prevention-trial participants were over 75 — so the evidence base here is genuinely thinner than for most of medicine. The trials that exist:

Why the usual risk calculator misleads you here

The Pooled Cohort Equations were built on populations that barely include anyone over 75. Past that age, they're dominated by the age term alone, ignore frailty/cognition/comorbidity, and ignore the competing risk of non-cardiovascular death — so they systematically overestimate the benefit of starting a statin in a frail 85-year-old.

High-Yield · CAC scoring as the tie-breaker

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring can refine the decision when risk is genuinely uncertain: CAC = 0 identifies a very-low-risk patient in whom it's reasonable to withhold or even deprescribe a primary-prevention statin. A high CAC score pushes the decision the other way. This is the single most useful "extra test" in this whole topic.

Putting a number on the decision: time-to-benefit vs life expectancy

The decision rule, stated plainly: if life expectancy < time-to-benefit of the intervention, it's more likely to harm than help and generally shouldn't be started. If they're roughly equal, patient preference should decide. Estimating life expectancy is itself a skill:

The point isn't "never treat the frail" — it's "decide deliberately, with the patient, using real data."

If you start one: monitoring that's actually evidence-based

  • Baseline: liver function tests and a comprehensive symptom check for pre-existing musculoskeletal complaints (so you have something to compare against — myalgia is extremely common in older adults at baseline, statin or not).
  • Routine LFT monitoring is no longer required (FDA removed this in 2012) — check only if symptoms/signs of hepatotoxicity appear.
  • Creatine kinase only for severe muscle symptoms or weakness during treatment, not routinely.
  • LDL-C recheck at 4–12 weeks after starting/adjusting dose, then every 3–12 months — no age-based distinction.
  • A1C if the patient has diabetes risk factors (statins modestly raise new-onset T2DM risk — comparable in older and younger patients, and outweighed by ASCVD benefit in most analyses).

Deprescribing — what actually happens when you stop

This is the part most clinicians get wrong by instinct: stopping a statin is not "safe by default." Three large observational cohorts (France, Denmark, Italy; combined N>200,000) found statin discontinuation in primary-prevention patients was associated with a ~30% relative increase in ASCVD events over the following 2–5 years compared with continuing. In the Danish cohort, that worked out to roughly 1 excess major cardiovascular event per 112 people who discontinued, per year.

High-Yield · but at the actual end of life, stopping helps

Kutner et al. randomized 381 people with life-limiting illness and <1 year estimated survival (36% in hospice) to continue vs discontinue their statin. No difference in 60-day survival or cardiovascular events — but quality of life improved and medication costs fell in the group that stopped. The two findings aren't contradictory — they describe different patients: discontinuation carries real cardiovascular cost in relatively well primary-prevention patients, but is genuinely beneficial (less burden, same survival) once life expectancy is truly limited. Knowing which patient is in front of you is the entire skill.

Going Further · how patients actually want this conversation framed

Surveyed clinicians prioritize talking about goals of therapy; surveyed older patients more often prefer hearing about risk of side effects as the reason to stop. A qualitative study of clinicians, caregivers, and patients with dementia found people preferred explanations like: "Our bodies change over time and certain medicines may no longer be needed," or "These medications take years to have an effect, and I think we should focus on what can help you right now." Worth having ready, verbatim, for your own deprescribing conversations.

Trap · two mirror-image errors

Over-treating: starting a primary-prevention statin in a frail 88-year-old with 2-year life expectancy because a calculator said "high risk" (it always will — age dominates the input). Exam Q · start, continue, or stop?

A robust, independent 81-year-old with prior MI is doing well on atorvastatin and asks if he can stop "now that he's old." Your advice?

Continue it. This is secondary prevention (established ASCVD) — statin benefit is clear and age is not a reason to stop in a fit patient with good life expectancy. Deprescribing applies to primary prevention with limited life expectancy/frailty, not to robust patients with proven coronary disease.

2.6 · The PT lecturePhysiotherapy's role in fall prevention

Falls get their full syndrome work-up in Day 3 (risk factors, screening, the multifactorial assessment). Here is the piece that specifically belongs to physiotherapy: what exercise, prescribed how, actually prevents falls — because "encourage exercise" is not a prescription, and the evidence is far more specific than that.

Analogy · balance is a skill, not a trait

People assume balance simply declines with age like eyesight. It's closer to a skill — like typing — that atrophies with disuse and can be retrained at any age. A 90-year-old who practices standing on one leg gets better at standing on one leg. That retrainability is the entire rationale for PT-led fall prevention.

The exercise types, ranked by evidence

  • Balance training — the single highest-yield component. Progressive challenges to the base of support (tandem stance, single-leg stance, reducing hand support) retrain the vestibular and proprioceptive systems most directly linked to falls.
  • Strength training — particularly lower-limb and hip extensor strength, which predicts the ability to recover from a trip before it becomes a fall. Progressive resistance (bands, weights, or body-weight) 2–3×/week.
  • Functional/gait training — practicing the actual movements that cause falls (sit-to-stand, turning, stepping over obstacles, dual-tasking like walking while talking).
  • Aerobic exercise — supports the other three but has the weakest direct evidence for fall reduction on its own.
High-Yield · the two named programmes worth knowing by name
  • Otago Exercise Programme — an individually prescribed, progressive home-based strength + balance programme (typically 3×/week, ~30 min, plus a walking plan), delivered/reviewed by a physiotherapist over several visits. One of the best-evidenced fall-prevention interventions in the literature — reduces falls by roughly a third in trial populations.
  • Tai Chi — group-based, slow controlled weight-shifting and balance challenge. Strong evidence for fall reduction, plus the added benefit of being enjoyable and socially engaging (which drives the adherence that makes any exercise programme actually work).

When to refer to physiotherapy specifically

  • Positive fall-risk screen (TUG ≥12s, recent fall, gait/balance complaint) → PT referral for a individualized, progressive programme — generic pamphlets without supervision are far less effective than a PT-led plan.
  • Post-fall, post-hospitalization, or post-fracture → PT for gait retraining and confidence-rebuilding (fear of falling itself causes deconditioning and further falls — a self-reinforcing cycle PT is well placed to interrupt).
  • Frailty or sarcopenia (Day 2.7) → resistance-training component overlaps directly with the muscle-preservation prescription.
Trap · "stay seated to be safe"

Restricting activity out of fear of falling is one of the most common, well-intentioned, and counterproductive instincts from families and even clinicians. Inactivity accelerates the strength and balance loss that causes falls — the safe instinct is almost always the wrong one. The evidence-based answer is supervised, progressive activity, not rest.

Exam Q · which programme

An 80-year-old with two falls in the past year and reduced quad strength is referred for fall prevention. Which intervention has the strongest evidence base?

An individualized, progressive strength-and-balance programme (e.g., Otago) delivered by a physiotherapist — superior to generic advice or aerobic exercise alone, with the strongest fall-reduction evidence of any single intervention.

2.7 · Muscle is medicineNutrition, sarcopenia & cancer prevention — in depth

Sarcopenia is the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength/function. It affects roughly 10–27% of community-dwelling adults ≥60, drives falls, fractures, frailty, respiratory impairment, cognitive decline, loss of independence, and worse outcomes from almost every acute illness — including cancer treatment, which is why this lecture pairs the two topics.

Analogy · the savings account

Muscle is a retirement account you can't easily top up late. Every bed-rest day and every low-protein week is a withdrawal; resistance exercise + protein are the only deposits. By the time someone is acutely ill — including starting chemotherapy — they're spending savings they should have banked years earlier.

The cancer connection

Sarcopenia and cancer interact in both directions. Many older adults carry sarcopenic obesity — normal or high BMI masking critically low muscle mass — invisible unless you specifically assess strength/mass rather than weight alone. In oncology this matters acutely:

  • Sarcopenia independently predicts chemotherapy toxicity, since cytotoxic drugs are often dosed by body-surface-area, not muscle mass — a sarcopenic patient effectively gets overdosed relative to their lean tissue.
  • It predicts surgical complications, longer recovery, and reduced cancer-specific survival.
  • Cancer cachexia (a distinct, inflammation-driven wasting syndrome — anorexia, weight loss, systemic inflammation) compounds age-related sarcopenia and does not reverse with food alone; it needs treatment of the underlying malignancy and multimodal supportive care, not just calorie pushing.
  • "Prehabilitation" — structured exercise and protein optimization in the weeks before surgery or chemotherapy — is an emerging, increasingly evidence-supported strategy to blunt treatment-related muscle loss and improve outcomes.
High-Yield · the prescription, summarized

Resistance/strength training is the cornerstone intervention (more effective for muscle than aerobic exercise alone), paired with protein 1.0–1.2 (up to 1.5) g/kg/day, vitamin D 800–1000 IU/day, and a Mediterranean or DASH-style dietary pattern. Nutrition and exercise are complementary, not interchangeable — trials combining both outperform either alone.

Trap · the restrictive diet that backfires

Tight "renal," "cardiac," or "diabetic" diets in a frail, sarcopenic elder are a classic iatrogenic driver of further muscle loss. In a frail patient, the goal is function, not weight loss — liberalize the diet, prioritize protein and adequate calories over a perfect sodium or sugar number.

Exam Q · diagnose the stage

A 78-year-old scores 5/10 on SARC-F. Grip strength testing is reduced. DXA shows low appendicular muscle mass. Gait speed is normal. What is the EWGSOP2 classification?

Confirmed sarcopenia (not severe). Low strength = probable sarcopenia; adding low muscle mass on DXA = confirmed. Severe would additionally require low physical performance (e.g., slow gait speed), which is normal here.

2.8 · The human skill, with the protocol behind itCommunicating diagnosis & caregiver support — in depth

The diagnosis is only half the encounter; how you deliver it, and what you build around it afterward, determines whether the plan actually happens. The detailed protocol below comes from formal dementia-disclosure guidelines, but the skeleton — prepare, ask permission, disclose gradually, plan follow-up, support the dyad — transfers to breaking any serious diagnosis in family medicine.

Before the appointment

  • Gauge the patient's and family's current understanding and attitudes about what's been happening; address misconceptions before adding new information.
  • Use ask-tell-ask even at the testing stage: "Do you have any questions about the memory testing we've done?" → brief, honest information → "What questions do you have about what that might mean?"
  • For uncertain results, it's appropriate to say plainly: "I cannot exclude Alzheimer's disease or dementia from this test, so we'll need to do some further testing." Approaching the diagnosis gradually — first as a possibility, later with more certainty — is a legitimate, recommended strategy, not evasion.
  • Plan sufficient time and a setting free of interruptions; a longer-than-usual appointment is preferable to a rushed one.

Delivering it

  • Signal that bad news is coming before stating it (the SPIKES "warning shot").
  • Have the carer/family present by default — but this is judged case-by-case against the patient's relationship and expressed wishes, not automatic.
  • Name the specific diagnosis (dementia, or the specific subtype) rather than a vague "memory problem" — clarity respects the patient's right to understand their own health, and vague language often causes more anxiety, not less.
  • Explain it as a brain condition so the physical basis for cognitive and behavioural change is clear.
  • Give only the immediately necessary information; deliberately delay non-urgent decisions to a follow-up visit. People cannot absorb a full care plan in the same breath as a diagnosis.
  • Emphasize what remains true and hopeful in an honest way: progression is often slow, symptomatic treatments exist, and active research continues — without overpromising a cure.
High-Yield · the right to know, and the right not to

People have both the right to know their diagnosis and the right not to know it. If a patient with capacity clearly states they don't want to be told, respect that — while ensuring the family/carers are supported to manage the practical consequences (e.g., driving, legal/financial decisions). If carers ask you to withhold the diagnosis against the patient's own wishes, that conflict is resolved through further conversation over time, not by simply complying with whichever party asked last.

Responding to the emotional reaction

  • Allow time to process — don't fill silence with more facts.
  • Acknowledge the emotion explicitly, in both patient and carer: anger, denial, shock, fear, or even relief (a diagnosis finally explaining months of unexplained change) are all normal and expected reactions.
  • Balance hope and realism — name preserved abilities specifically, not just generic reassurance.
  • Encourage continued social engagement and lifestyle measures (Mediterranean diet, exercise, social activity) as part of an ongoing-care narrative, not a one-time piece of advice.
Trap · missing the safety-net question

People with a prior history of depression or self-harm carry an elevated risk of depression, self-harm, or suicidal ideation in the months following a dementia diagnosis. This reaction is believed to be uncommon but is a recognized risk that's easy to miss amid the logistics of a new diagnosis. Build in a deliberate check on mood and safety at this visit and the follow-up, and have a low threshold to offer counselling or a mental-health care plan alongside the dementia work-up — this is a named, formal recommendation, not just good instinct.

The follow-up structure — what goes in which visit

Disclosure is never a single conversation. The recommended structure spreads information across 2–3 follow-up consultations, with a review scheduled within 2 months of the specialist diagnosis:

Visit 1 — disclosure

Visits 2–3 — build-out

Ongoing communication as the disease progresses — the actual technique

People with dementia retain the desire to communicate even as language fails, and their grasp of emotion and tone persists longer than their grasp of words — so how you sound matters more than what you say. A practical, evidence-informed toolkit:

  • Non-verbal: face-to-face, same eye level, comfortable eye contact, gestures to support meaning.
  • Verbal: short sentences of 4–6 words, one verb each, active voice; one idea and one question at a time; pause and wait (allow ≥5 seconds before expecting a response — don't rush or interrupt slowness).
  • Avoid metaphors, colloquialisms, and pronouns (poor working memory breaks the link between "she/he/it" and who you mean) — use names directly.
  • "Wh" questions (what, who, where, when) are easier to process than "why" or "how"; follow with yes/no questions if needed.
  • Don't quiz or argue if the person seems confused — acknowledge the feeling behind their words and redirect gently, rather than correcting them outright.
  • If they can't find a word: suggest one, repeat the unfinished sentence with a likely word, or ask "Do you mean…?" — then repeat/rephrase if still not understood.
  • Use familiar, interesting material (photos, memorabilia) to sustain engagement and rapport.
High-Yield · validate, don't correct

When a patient with dementia repeats a question or asserts something untrue (e.g., "when is my wife arriving" about a wife who has passed), the evidence-based response is to repeat part of what they said and add gently to it, or redirect with warmth — not to correct, argue, or quiz them on facts they've already lost access to. This single principle resolves most of the difficult communication scenarios you'll be tested on or face in clinic.

Cultural and language considerations

Use professional interpreters or bilingual/bicultural staff throughout the pathway, especially during assessment, diagnosis disclosure, and consent — not just when a language barrier becomes obviously unmanageable. For Indigenous and culturally/linguistically diverse communities specifically, services should be culturally informed, involve family and community representatives in care planning, and recognize that decision-making in many cultures is family-centred rather than strictly individual — directly relevant in this region. This doesn't override the patient's right to know; it shapes how that right is exercised.

Exam Q · the safety-net you might miss

A 74-year-old with a history of major depression is newly diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease. Beyond the standard disclosure protocol, what specific additional step is recommended?

Explicitly assess mood and safety, and have a low threshold to offer counselling (or a mental-health care plan / psychogeriatric referral). Patients with a prior history of depression or self-harm carry an elevated risk of depression, self-harm, or suicidal ideation in the months following a dementia diagnosis — a formally recognized risk, not just clinical intuition.

Day 3 · Tuesday

Medication safety & the brain

The two engines of safe prescribing (Beers + STOPP/START), the deprescribing mindset, the 3 Ds, and the falls–frailty axis that ties it together.

3.1 · The mindsetDeprescribing principles

Deprescribing is the planned, supervised reduction or stopping of medicines that may be causing harm or no longer serving the patient. It is active care, not giving up. The backdrop: polypharmacy (≥5 medications), where each drug was reasonable alone but the combination drives falls, delirium, and adverse drug events — implicated in roughly 30% of older-adult hospital admissions.

Analogy · 30 browser tabs

Each medication is a browser tab opened for a good reason. Open thirty and the whole system slows, crashes, and overheats — and nobody remembers why half are still running. Deprescribing is closing the tabs you no longer need so the ones that matter run properly.

The prescribing cascade — spot it and stop it

A drug causes a side-effect → the side-effect is mistaken for a new disease → a second drug is added to treat it. Example: a CCB causes ankle oedema → a diuretic is added → the diuretic causes incontinence → an anticholinergic bladder drug is added → the anticholinergic causes confusion and a fall. Four drugs, one original problem.

High-Yield · the deprescribing sequence
  1. Reconcile — a full "brown-bag" review of everything (Rx, OTC, herbal).
  2. Identify targets — no indication, no benefit, duplicated, not taken, or a PIM (use Beers + STOPP).
  3. Prioritize — start with the highest-harm, lowest-benefit drug, weighed against life expectancy and What Matters.
  4. Taper where withdrawal is a risk — benzodiazepines, opioids, gabapentinoids, β-blockers, PPIs, antidepressants — never stop these abruptly.
  5. Monitor — watch for withdrawal or return of the original condition.
Trap · stopping cold turkey

Benzodiazepines stopped abruptly → withdrawal seizures; long-term β-blockers → rebound tachycardia/ischemia; SSRIs → discontinuation syndrome; PPIs → rebound hyperacidity. Deprescribing ≠ abrupt cessation. Taper, and pick one drug at a time so you can attribute any change.

3.2 · Engine oneThe AGS Beers Criteria®

An explicit, consensus list of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in adults ≥65. It's organized into five tables: (1) PIMs to avoid in most older adults · (2) drug–disease/syndrome interactions · (3) drugs to use with caution · (4) drug–drug interactions to avoid · (5) drugs needing renal dose adjustment.

High-Yield · the "usually avoid" all-stars
  • First-generation antihistamines (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, chlorpheniramine) — anticholinergic.
  • Benzodiazepines & "Z-drugs" (zolpidem) — falls, fractures, delirium, cognition.
  • Anticholinergic antidepressants (amitriptyline, imipramine, paroxetine).
  • Antipsychotics — avoid for BPSD/insomnia unless approved indication; ↑ stroke & mortality in dementia.
  • Sliding-scale insulin & long-acting sulfonylureas (glyburide/glibenclamide) — hypoglycemia.
  • Skeletal muscle relaxants · chronic NSAIDs · PPIs >8 weeks (without indication).
  • Peripheral α-1 blockers (doxazosin) for HTN · IR nifedipine · digoxin >0.125 mg/day.
  • Antispasmodics & bladder antimuscarinics (oxybutynin) — anticholinergic burden.
Analogy · the anticholinergic overdraft

Think of acetylcholine as a bank balance the aging brain keeps low. Every anticholinergic drug — that antihistamine, that TCA, that bladder drug, that antipsychotic — is a withdrawal. Stack a few "small" ones and the account overdraws into the classic syndrome: "blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare" — plus urinary retention, constipation, falls, and confusion. Burden is cumulative — it's the total, not any single drug, that tips the patient over.

High-Yield · drug–disease landmines (Beers Table 2)
  • Heart failure (reduced EF): avoid non-DHP CCBs (diltiazem, verapamil) & NSAIDs.
  • Delirium / dementia: avoid anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, H2 blockers (cimetidine), antipsychotics.
  • History of falls/fractures: avoid benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, antipsychotics, TCAs, SSRIs/SNRIs, antiepileptics, opioids.
  • Parkinson's: avoid metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, most antipsychotics (quetiapine/clozapine/pimavanserin OK).
Trap · Beers is a flag, not a ban

Beers identifies drugs whose risk usually outweighs benefit — it's a prompt to review and justify, not an absolute prohibition. A short course of an "avoid" drug for a clear indication can be appropriate. The error is reflexive prescribing of a PIM and reflexive stopping without thought. Also: don't treat Beers as covering everything — STOPP catches drug–drug and disease-context issues Beers misses, and START reminds you of under-prescribing.

3.4 · The cognitive triadThe 3 Ds: delirium · dementia · depression

Telling these three apart is the most-tested judgement in geriatrics — and the most consequential, because the management could not be more different. They also overlap: delirium is frequently superimposed on dementia, and depression can mimic both.

Analogy · sunset, power-cut, fog

Dementia is a slow sunset — a gradual, irreversible dimming over years. Delirium is a sudden power-cut — abrupt, fluctuating, and you must hunt for the tripped breaker (it's usually fixable). Depression is a fog — it rolls in over weeks, the lights still work but everything feels effortful, and it lifts with treatment.

High-Yield · the two discriminators

1. Inattention + acute fluctuation = delirium until proven otherwise — it's a medical emergency, not a baseline. 2. "Pseudodementia" = depression masquerading as dementia: the depressed patient complains of memory loss and answers "I don't know," whereas the demented patient conceals deficits and confabulates near-misses. When unsure, treat the depression and re-test cognition — improvement reveals the answer.

3.5 · The emergencyDelirium — acute confusional state

Delirium is acute brain failure — and it's common, dangerous, and largely preventable. One-third of medical inpatients over 70 develop it; up to 80% in the ICU. It is not benign: it triples mortality, lengthens stay by up to 10 days, and leaves ~25% with lasting cognitive deficits comparable to mild Alzheimer's. In the ICU, one-year survival falls roughly 10% for every day of delirium.

Three faces — and the dangerous one is the quiet one

  • Hyperactive (~5%) — agitated, hallucinating; easy to spot.
  • Hypoactive (~30%) — withdrawn, drowsy, "pleasantly confused." Most missed, worst prognosis (mortality ~33% vs ~15% hyperactive).
  • Mixed (~65%) — fluctuates between the two.
Trap · the quiet patient is not "doing fine"

The hypoactive patient who is calm and sleepy gets overlooked precisely because they're not causing trouble — and they're the one most likely to die. This is why you screen everyone (CAM / UB-CAM, q12h in hospital), not just the agitated.

High-Yield · prevention beats treatment (and there's no cure drug)

Multicomponent non-pharmacologic prevention (Inouye/HELP) cuts delirium incidence by ~44%: reorient (clocks, calendars, glasses, hearing aids), protect the sleep-wake cycle, mobilize early, treat pain ("analgesia first"), hydrate & nourish, and avoid polypharmacy. There is no approved medication to treat delirium — and most psychoactive drugs make it worse.

High-Yield · when (rarely) you must medicate

Drugs are only for severe agitation that endangers the patient or others, or for productive psychotic symptoms — never as routine sedation. Then: low-dose haloperidol or a low-dose atypical (quetiapine), with cardiac monitoring (QT). Avoid benzodiazepines — they worsen delirium — except in alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal, where they are the treatment. Remember the TA-DA approach: Tolerate, Anticipate, Don't Agitate.

Exam Q · the agitated post-op patient

A 78-year-old is agitated and pulling at lines on post-op day 1. After ensuring safety, what is the correct first management step?

Search for and treat the underlying cause (pain, urinary retention, hypoxia, infection, drug effect, electrolytes) and apply non-pharmacologic measures (reorient, glasses/hearing aids, family, mobilize, protect sleep). Medication is a last resort for danger only — and never a benzodiazepine unless this is withdrawal.
Going Further

Screening tools by setting: CAM-ICU / ICDSC (ventilated/ICU), 3D-CAM & Nu-DESC (general ward), CAM-S (adds severity). Perioperative dexmedetomidine reduces postoperative delirium (OR ~0.35), but routine prophylactic antipsychotics are not supported. The pathophysiology — neuroinflammation, cholinergic deficiency, and stress-axis dysregulation on a vulnerable (often demented) brain — explains why anticholinergics are so deliriogenic.

3.6 · The slow sunsetDementia

Dementia is acquired, progressive decline in ≥1 cognitive domain that impairs function — that last part is what separates it from normal aging and mild cognitive impairment. Up to half of cases go undiagnosed, so screen on any concern from the patient or family.

The four common dementias — the distinguishing fingerprint

Work-up — first, exclude the reversible mimics

Screen (Mini-Cog or GPCOG) → if positive, characterize (MoCA <26 · SLUMS ≤19). Then rule out treatable causes before labeling a progressive dementia: depression (pseudodementia), B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism (TSH), medication effect, and normal-pressure hydrocephalus (the triad — "wet, wobbly, wacky": urinary incontinence, gait apraxia, cognitive decline). Neuroimaging (CT/MRI) screens for structural/vascular causes and subdural.

High-Yield · treatment, honestly
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) — mild–moderate Alzheimer's; rivastigmine also for Lewy body/Parkinson's dementia. Modest symptomatic benefit, not disease-modifying.
  • Memantine (NMDA antagonist) — moderate–severe; can combine with a cholinesterase inhibitor.
  • BPSD (agitation, psychosis): non-pharmacologic first — the DICE approach (Describe, Investigate, Create, Evaluate). Reserve antipsychotics for severe/dangerous/refractory symptoms, lowest dose, shortest time (↑ stroke & mortality).
Trap · cholinesterase inhibitors & the heart

STOPP flags cholinesterase inhibitors in persistent bradycardia (<60), heart block, or with rate-limiting drugs (β-blockers, digoxin, diltiazem/verapamil) — risk of syncope & falls. And in Lewy body dementia, conventional antipsychotics can be life-threatening (severe sensitivity) — use quetiapine/clozapine/pimavanserin if absolutely needed.

Disclosure honours the patient's right to know (Alzheimer Society): inform sensitively, identify a support person to be present, cover prognosis, treatment, and support — and use the diagnosis as the trigger for advance care planning while capacity remains. Honour an explicit wish not to know. And remember: dementia care is a dyad — support the caregiver as deliberately as the patient (see Day 2).

3.7 · The geriatric syndromeFalls & frailty

One-third of community-dwellers ≥65 fall each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in those ≥75. A fall is rarely one thing — it's a multifactorial event, and the single strongest predictor of the next fall is the last one (especially within 3 months).

Analogy · the last straw

A fall is the last straw on an already overloaded camel: weak quadriceps, orthostatic drop, a sedating drug, dim vision, a urinary urge, and a loose rug. No single straw caused it — which is why effective prevention removes several straws at once (multifactorial), not just one.

Risk factors

Intrinsic (the patient)Extrinsic (the environment)
Previous fall · age · polypharmacy (≥4 drugs) · psychotropics (benzos, sedatives, antipsychotics, antidepressants, antihypertensives) · muscle weakness & gait/balance impairment · orthostatic hypotension · poor vision · urinary urgency/incontinence · cognitive impairment (dementia/delirium)Poor lighting · loose rugs/cords · slippery/wet floors · stairs without rails · ill-fitting footwear · no grab bars in bathroom · unfamiliar/new environment (hospital admission)
High-Yield · screen & assess (AGS/BGS)

Ask every older adult about falls at least once a year. If they report a fall (or a gait/balance problem, or a positive TUG ≥12 s), do a multifactorial assessment. In hospital, use a validated tool (Morse Fall Scale — <25 low, 25–45 moderate, >45 high). The point of a score isn't the number — it's identifying which modifiable risk factors to act on.

High-Yield · the interventions that actually work
  • Exercise — strongest evidence: combined strength + balance training (Tai Chi, Otago programme). This is the headline intervention.
  • Medication review / deprescribe the psychotropics and orthostasis-causing drugs.
  • Vitamin D in those who are deficient.
  • Home hazard modification (OT-led, especially after a fall).
  • Vision correction; treat orthostatic hypotension; safe footwear; manage continence.
  • Treat osteoporosis — you can't prevent every fall, so reduce the fracture when one happens.

Frailty — the master syndrome

Frailty is a state of depleted reserve that makes minor stressors cause disproportionate decline. Two models you should know:

  • Fried physical phenotype — 5 criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness (grip), slow gait speed, low physical activity. ≥3 = frail · 1–2 = pre-frail.
  • Deficit-accumulation — the Rockwood Frailty Index / Clinical Frailty Scale (1–9): frailty as the count of accumulated deficits.
Analogy · the frayed rope

Two ropes hold the same weight: one new, one frayed. Add a small tug and only the frayed one snaps. Frailty is the fraying — which is why a frail elder decompensates from a UTI that a fit peer shrugs off, and why frailty (not age) should set how aggressively you treat, screen, and prescribe.

High-Yield · frailty is (partly) reversible

The same levers run through everything in this course: resistance exercise + adequate protein (≥1.2 g/kg/day) + vitamin D, plus deprescribing and treating reversible contributors via a CGA. Gait speed alone (<0.8 m/s) is a fast, powerful frailty signal — the "sixth vital sign."

Exam Q · the recurrent faller

An 80-year-old on zolpidem and doxazosin has fallen twice this year. Beyond a multifactorial assessment, what two medication actions give the biggest risk reduction?

Deprescribe the zolpidem (Z-drug → sedation/falls) and stop/switch the doxazosin (α-blocker → orthostatic hypotension). Both are Beers/STOPP fall-risk PIMs. Pair with strength + balance exercise and a home-hazard review for the largest combined effect.

4.1 · The final priorityEnd-of-life care & advance care planning

This is where What Matters becomes the whole plan. The skill is shifting the goal — from cure to comfort and meaning — before a crisis forces the decision in an ED at 3 a.m.

High-Yield · the advance-care-planning toolkit
  • Do it early — while the patient has capacity (especially at a dementia diagnosis).
  • Advance directive / living will — the patient's stated wishes.
  • Durable power of attorney / healthcare proxy — who decides if they can't.

High-Yield · surrogate decision-making hierarchy

When the patient can't decide: use substituted judgment first — decide as the patient would have, based on their known values/wishes. Only when those are unknown do you fall back to the best-interest standard. A surrogate's job is to channel the patient, not to substitute their own preference.

Symptom management at the end of life

  • Pain & dyspnea: opioids are the mainstay — titrate to effect; fear of addiction is misplaced at end of life. Always co-prescribe a laxative (tolerance to constipation never develops). Oxygen only if hypoxic.
  • Terminal secretions ("death rattle"): reposition, reassure family; antimuscarinics if needed.
  • Terminal delirium / agitation, nausea, anxiety: treat reversible causes where consistent with goals; symptom-directed medication.
Trap · culture is part of the plan, not an obstacle

In this region especially, decisions are often family-centred and shaped by faith. Explore disclosure preferences, involve the family the patient wants involved, and integrate religious/cultural practices into comfort care. "Patient autonomy" doesn't mean isolating the patient from their family — it means honouring how this patient wants decisions made.

Going Further

Prognostication is a learnable skill — tools like ePrognosis and disease-specific indices beat clinician gestalt (which is reliably over-optimistic). Know the evidence that early palliative care improves quality of life and, in some cancers, even survival (Temel et al., NEJM 2010) — palliative care is not "giving up," and integrating it early is best practice.

4.2 · Fuel for a failing bodyRecognizing & treating malnutrition

Malnutrition in older adults is common, under-recognized, and a powerful predictor of falls, infection, delayed healing, longer admissions, and death — locked in a vicious cycle with sarcopenia (see Day 2). Screen actively; weight loss is never "just aging."

High-Yield · screen & size it up

Use the MNA (Mini Nutritional Assessment): 12–14 normal · 8–11 at risk · ≤7 malnourished (or MUST). Red flags: unintentional weight loss (≥5% in a month / ≥10% in 6 months), low BMI, poor intake, sarcopenia.

Why are they not eating? — MEALS ON WHEELS

The classic mnemonic for reversible causes of weight loss in elders: Medications · Emotional (depression) · Anorexia/Alcohol · Late-life paranoia · Swallowing problems · Oral/dental problems · No money · Wandering (dementia) · Hyper/hypo-metabolism (thyroid) · Enteric (malabsorption) · Eating difficulties · Low-salt/low-cholesterol restrictive diets · Social isolation / shopping problems.

High-Yield · how to treat it (in order)
  1. Fix the reversible cause (depression, oral/dental, dysphagia, meds causing anorexia, social/financial barriers).
  2. Liberalize the diet — drop restrictive "therapeutic" diets; food first: fortify, offer frequent small energy-dense meals, give eating assistance, make mealtimes social.
  3. Adequate protein (1.0–1.2, up to 1.5 g/kg/day if ill) + vitamin D (800–1000 IU) + resistance exercise — the anti-sarcopenia package.
  4. Oral nutritional supplements when food alone falls short (between meals, so they don't replace it).
  5. Watch for refeeding syndrome (hypophosphatemia) when re-nourishing the severely depleted — go slow, replace electrolytes.
Trap · the feeding tube in advanced dementia

One of the most important EOL teaching points: in advanced dementia, PEG/tube feeding does not prolong survival, prevent aspiration, heal pressure ulcers, or improve comfort — and adds burden (restraints, infections, agitation). Careful hand-feeding / comfort feeding is preferred. This is a frequent exam item and a real-world family conversation — the answer is comfort feeding, not a tube.

Exam Q · the family request

The family of a patient with end-stage dementia who is eating little asks for a feeding tube "so she doesn't starve." Best response?

Recommend careful hand-feeding / comfort feeding rather than a PEG tube. In advanced dementia, tube feeding does not improve survival, aspiration, pressure ulcers, or comfort, and increases harm. Explore the family's fears with empathy, reframe around comfort and What Matters, and treat any reversible contributor — but the evidence-based answer is comfort feeding.

BPSD approach: Describe · Investigate · Create a plan · Evaluate — non-pharmacologic first.

SPIKES

Breaking bad news: Setting · Perception · Invitation · Knowledge · Empathy · Strategy.

Renal stops

NSAIDs <50 · nitrofurantoin <45 · metformin/dabigatran/MRA <30 · Xa inhibitors <15 · colchicine <10.

DM targets

A1C <7.0–7.5 (healthy) → <8.0 (complex) → none (poor health). BP 130/80 → 130/80 → 140/90.

Cognitive cut-offs

MoCA <26 · SLUMS ≤19 (dementia) · Mini-Cog: recall 0 = +, 3 = −, 1–2 → clock decides.

Mobility numbers

TUG ≥12 s flags fall risk · gait speed <0.8 m/s slow, <0.6 high risk · Morse >45 high.

Frailty (Fried)

Weight loss · exhaustion · weakness · slow gait · low activity. ≥3 frail, 1–2 pre-frail.

NPH triad

"Wet, wobbly, wacky" — incontinence, gait apraxia, cognitive decline (a reversible mimic).

Protein target

1.0–1.2 g/kg/day healthy · ≥1.2 (up to 1.5) if sarcopenic/ill · vitamin D 800–1000 IU.

Vaccines (START)

Influenza yearly · pneumococcal ≥once · herpes zoster · SARS-CoV-2.

That's the full four-day arc — assessment → chronic disease → medication safety & the brain → end of life. Run the 4Ms on every older adult, individualize by frailty rather than age, deprescribe with intent, and let What Matters set the destination. Good luck on the MCQ.

One idea to carry out of the course

Geriatric medicine is not a smaller version of adult medicine — it is a re-ordering of priorities. The disease list stops being the agenda and becomes one input. What Matters sets the destination; Medication, Mentation, and Mobility are how you protect the journey. Run that checklist on every older adult, in every setting, every time, and you are already practising age-friendly care.
