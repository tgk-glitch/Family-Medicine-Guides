Mental health · primary care

Depression and anxiety present in every clinic

Often through the body first — as fatigue, chest tightness, headache, or pain that keeps returning with normal investigations. Seven topics, each on the same clinical skeleton: at a glance → red flags → features → diagnosis → differential → management → special situations → prevention → complications → disposition → prognosis → follow-up → what to tell the patient → local context.

Two things run through all of it: stigma is the dominant barrier to care in this region, shaping how patients present and whether they return; and somatic expression of distress is a normal cultural idiom here, not in itself a disorder — which changes how you interpret what you're hearing.

Depression

The commonest mental health presentation in primary care — and often arriving through the body

◍At a glance

  • What it is: a syndrome of persistently low mood and/or anhedonia with neurovegetative and cognitive features, lasting ≥2 weeks and impairing function.
  • How common: among the leading causes of disability worldwide; roughly 1 in 10 primary care attenders meet criteria at any time, and a substantial share are never identified.
  • Why it matters here: presentation is frequently somatic and stigma is the dominant barrier to care — so the detection rate depends almost entirely on whether the clinician asks.
🚩 Red flags
  • Suicidal ideation, plan, or intent — assess immediately (see suicide risk).
  • Psychotic features — delusions, hallucinations, especially mood-congruent nihilistic or guilt themes.
  • Refusal of food and fluid, or catatonia.
  • Severe self-neglect, or inability to care for dependent children.
  • Postpartum depression with any thought of harm to self or the infant — urgent.
  • New severe depression in an older adult with cognitive decline — exclude organic cause and consider high suicide risk in this group.

A positive item 9 requires direct assessment now, whatever the total. A low total does not make it safe to defer. Go to suicide risk assessment.

If this is about you: please talk to someone today — 920033360 (NCMH counselling), or 997 / 911 in an emergency.

◐Clinical features

  • Core: depressed mood, and/or loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia). At least one must be present.
  • SIG E CAPS: Sleep change · Interest loss · Guilt/worthlessness · Energy loss · Concentration difficulty · Appetite/weight change · Psychomotor change (retardation or agitation) · Suicidal ideation.
  • Somatic presentations are the norm, not the exception — fatigue, headache, back or abdominal pain, palpitations, dizziness. Many patients never volunteer a mood word.
  • In older adults: may present as cognitive complaint, apathy, or somatic preoccupation rather than sadness.
  • In adolescents: irritability rather than sadness is common.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis: ≥5 symptoms for ≥2 weeks, at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia, causing impairment, not better explained by another cause.
  • Screen with PHQ-2 (mood + anhedonia); if positive, complete the PHQ-9 above.
  • PHQ-9 is a severity measure, not a diagnosis — it grades and tracks, it does not replace assessment.
  • Baseline investigations to exclude mimics: CBC, TSH, ferritin, vitamin D, B12, glucose/HbA1c, renal and liver function. Toxicology and pregnancy test where relevant.
  • Always screen for bipolarity before prescribing — DIG FAST (Distractibility, Indiscretion, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness).

✚Management

  • Mild–moderate: psychotherapy (CBT) first-line. Behavioural activation, structured exercise, sleep regulation and social reconnection are genuinely effective, not filler.
  • Moderate–severe: antidepressant plus psychotherapy beats either alone.
  • First-line agents: SSRIs — sertraline, escitalopram. Start low; review at 1–2 weeks for tolerability and safety; titrate at 2–4 weeks if response inadequate.
  • Set the timeline at the first visit: some benefit 2–4 weeks, fuller response 6–8 weeks. Warn about transient early nausea, headache and anxiety, or they stop on day four.
  • Duration: continue 6–12 months after remission for a first episode; longer if recurrent.
  • Discontinuation: taper gradually — abrupt stopping causes discontinuation syndrome that patients misread as relapse or addiction.
  • Switching: if no response at an adequate dose by 6–8 weeks, switch within or across class. Two adequate failed trials defines treatment resistance.

◈Special situations

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: sertraline usually preferred in both. Untreated maternal depression carries substantial risk — do not default to stopping.
  • Older adults: start lower, go slower; watch hyponatraemia with SSRIs, falls, and interactions. Depression is a reversible contributor to apparent cognitive decline — see Geriatrics.
  • Adolescents: fluoxetine has the best evidence; psychotherapy first for mild disease; monitor closely early in treatment.
  • Cardiac disease: sertraline is the best-studied and generally preferred.
  • Ramadan: most antidepressants can be consolidated to once-daily dosing at iftar or suhoor. Discuss before Ramadan — abrupt self-discontinuation during the month is a common and avoidable cause of relapse.

⊘Prevention

  • Relapse prevention is the main achievable goal — continuing treatment for the full course after remission is the single most effective step.
  • Maintenance psychotherapy or mindfulness-based cognitive therapy reduces recurrence in those with multiple episodes.
  • Address modifiable contributors: sleep, alcohol, physical inactivity, social isolation, untreated pain, and untreated comorbid anxiety.
  • Treat perinatal risk actively — history of depression is the strongest predictor of postpartum depression; plan monitoring before delivery.
  • Population level: reducing adverse childhood experiences, and early treatment of anxiety in adolescence, both reduce later depression.

⚠Complications

  • Suicide and self-harm.
  • Chronic course and treatment resistance — risk increases with each untreated episode.
  • Functional loss: work, education, relationships, parenting capacity.
  • Worse outcomes in comorbid physical illness — depression reduces adherence and independently worsens cardiovascular prognosis.
  • Substance use as self-medication.
  • In the perinatal period: impaired bonding, and effects on infant development.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care: mild–moderate depression without suicidality, psychosis, or bipolarity, with reliable follow-up.
  • Refer to psychiatry: suicidal ideation with intent or plan; psychotic features; suspected bipolar disorder; treatment resistance (two adequate trials); severe functional impairment; pregnancy or postpartum with significant severity; adolescent with moderate–severe illness.
  • Emergency/admission: imminent suicide risk, catatonia, refusal of food and fluid, or inability to maintain basic safety.

↗Prognosis

  • Most single episodes remit — the majority of treated patients improve substantially within weeks to months.
  • Recurrence is the rule rather than the exception: risk rises with each episode, and residual symptoms are the strongest predictor of relapse — aim for full remission, not partial improvement.
  • Untreated episodes typically last many months and may become chronic; earlier treatment shortens the episode.
  • Poorer prognosis with: residual symptoms, comorbid anxiety or substance use, chronic physical illness, ongoing psychosocial adversity, and early age of onset.

↻Follow-up

  • 1–2 weeks after starting or changing an antidepressant — tolerability and safety, particularly in the young where early monitoring matters.
  • Then every 2–4 weeks until response, using the PHQ-9 to track rather than relying on recall.
  • Monthly once improving, through the first several months.
  • Then 3-monthly through the continuation phase, and at any dose change or taper.
  • Re-screen for suicidality at every visit — risk can rise transiently as energy returns before mood does.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "This is a medical condition, not a weakness or a lack of faith — and it responds to treatment."
  • "The medication is not addictive, and it does not change who you are."
  • "You will probably feel some benefit in two to four weeks, and more by six to eight. The first week can feel slightly worse before it feels better — that is expected and it passes."
  • "We continue it for months after you feel well, because stopping early is the commonest reason people relapse."
  • "If thoughts of harming yourself come, tell me or come back — that is not a reason to be ashamed and it is something we can help with."

⌖Local context

  • Stigma dominates. It shapes presentation, acceptance of diagnosis, whether the prescription is filled, and whether the patient returns. Naming it openly and non-judgementally often does more than any leaflet.
  • Framing depression as a treatable biological illness — as you would anemia or hypothyroidism — is both accurate and more acceptable to many patients than a psychological framing.
  • Family involvement is usually an asset here, but ask permission and clarify what the patient wants shared. Confidentiality concern is a documented reason people avoid public clinics.
  • Faith and religious practice are a genuine source of coping and meaning for many patients; engaging respectfully strengthens the alliance and is not in tension with treatment.
  • Ask non-judgementally about help already sought elsewhere, including spiritual healing — the concern is delayed presentation, not the practice.

Anxiety Disorders

Where the physical symptoms are real and the investigations keep coming back normal

◍At a glance

  • What it is: a family of disorders defined by excessive, persistent fear or worry that is disproportionate and functionally impairing — distinct from ordinary, adaptive anxiety.
  • How common: the most prevalent group of mental disorders; a large share of primary care attenders with unexplained physical symptoms have an underlying anxiety disorder.
  • Why it matters here: these are the patients who accumulate repeated normal investigations. Each negative result reassures briefly, then stops working — and the cycle itself becomes part of the illness.
🚩 Red flags
  • Suicidal ideation — anxiety disorders carry elevated risk, particularly with comorbid depression.
  • First presentation of 'anxiety' over age 40 without prior history — raise the threshold for an organic cause.
  • Syncope, chest pain with exertion, or documented arrhythmia — investigate as cardiac, not anxiety.
  • Weight loss, tremor, heat intolerance — exclude hyperthyroidism.
  • Escalating alcohol, sedative, or benzodiazepine use.
  • Complete avoidance — housebound, unable to work or attend school.

◐Clinical features

Panic disorderRecurrent unexpected panic attacks — abrupt surge peaking within minutes — plus ≥1 month of worry about further attacks or behavioural change to avoid them

Somatic presentation is the norm: chest pain, palpitations, dyspnea, dizziness, epigastric pain, globus, tremor, sweating, urinary frequency.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis against DSM-5 criteria for the specific disorder — the subtype matters because treatment differs.
  • GAD-7 above for screening and severity; ≥10 is the usual cut-off for probable GAD. It is a measure, not a diagnosis.
  • Investigations to exclude mimics: TSH, CBC, glucose, ferritin, vitamin D. ECG where palpitations or chest pain dominate — ideally captured during symptoms. Toxicology where indicated.
  • Take a caffeine and supplement history explicitly — coffee, energy drinks, pre-workout preparations. This is frequently the whole answer and is rarely volunteered.

⇄Differential diagnosis

✚Management

  • CBT is first-line for all anxiety disorders, with the most durable effect. Exposure-based work is essential for panic, social anxiety, and phobias.
  • Pharmacological first-line: SSRI or SNRI. Start at a lower dose than for depression — anxious patients are more sensitive to early activation, and a jittery first week ends adherence.
  • Timeline is longer than depression — meaningful response often 6–12 weeks. Say so upfront.
  • Duration: at least 12 months after response; relapse on early discontinuation is high.
  • Adjuncts with real evidence: regular aerobic exercise, sleep regulation, caffeine reduction, breathing retraining for panic.
  • ⚠ Benzodiazepines — the trap in this topic

    They work immediately, which is precisely the problem: rapid relief teaches the patient that the drug is what makes anxiety survivable, and undermines the exposure learning that produces durable recovery.

    • Not first-line and not for maintenance — tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, cognitive impairment, falls, driving risk.
    • If used at all: lowest dose, shortest course, explicit stop date agreed at the start, with SSRI/CBT already running.
    • Avoid in the elderly — falls and cognitive impairment; on the Beers Criteria (see Geriatrics).
    • Patients arriving already established on long-term benzodiazepines need a slow negotiated taper, not abrupt cessation.

◈Special situations

  • Pregnancy: CBT preferred where feasible. If medication is needed, sertraline is the usual choice; untreated severe anxiety carries its own obstetric risk.
  • Older adults: exclude physical causes first; avoid benzodiazepines; consider that new anxiety may herald depression, dementia, or medical illness.
  • Children and adolescents: CBT first-line; SSRIs where moderate–severe, with close monitoring.
  • Comorbid depression is present in a large proportion and changes the plan — treat both, and expect a slower response.
  • Ramadan: caffeine and sleep disruption both spike; anticipate a symptomatic worsening that is not treatment failure.

⊘Prevention

  • Early treatment of childhood and adolescent anxiety reduces adult anxiety and depression — this is one of the better-evidenced preventive opportunities in psychiatry.
  • Avoid iatrogenic maintenance: unnecessary investigation, excessive reassurance-seeking, and benzodiazepine initiation all entrench the disorder.
  • Relapse prevention: complete the full 12-month course; taper slowly; maintain the behavioural skills learned in CBT.
  • Address caffeine, alcohol, sleep deprivation and physical inactivity as modifiable maintaining factors.

⚠Complications

  • Substance use disorder, particularly alcohol and benzodiazepines, as self-medication.
  • Secondary depression — common, and worsens prognosis.
  • Occupational and educational failure; social withdrawal; in severe agoraphobia, becoming housebound.
  • Iatrogenic harm from repeated investigation, including incidental findings and radiation exposure.
  • Elevated suicide risk, particularly with comorbid depression or panic disorder.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care: most GAD, panic disorder and social anxiety, with CBT access and SSRI treatment.
  • Refer: suicidal ideation; failure of two adequate trials; severe functional impairment; suspected PTSD, OCD, or bipolar disorder; comorbid substance use disorder including benzodiazepine dependence; children and adolescents with moderate–severe illness.
  • Emergency: rarely required for anxiety itself — but a first panic attack with chest pain and cardiac risk factors is a cardiac assessment until proven otherwise.

↗Prognosis

  • Highly treatable — CBT and SSRIs both produce substantial improvement in the majority.
  • But naturally chronic and relapsing if untreated, often waxing and waning over years; spontaneous remission of GAD is uncommon.
  • Panic disorder responds particularly well to exposure-based CBT, with durable gains.
  • Worse prognosis with: comorbid depression or personality disorder, substance use, ongoing psychosocial adversity, and long duration before treatment.
  • Gains from CBT persist after treatment ends, which medication alone does not reliably achieve — a reason to push for therapy access.

↻Follow-up

  • 1–2 weeks after starting an SSRI — check for early activation, which is the commonest reason for discontinuation.
  • Every 2–4 weeks during titration, tracking with the GAD-7.
  • Monthly until stable response, remembering that 6–12 weeks may be needed.
  • 3-monthly during the maintenance year.
  • Review at any taper, and warn that a transient symptom rise during a taper is expected and not automatic evidence of relapse.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "Your symptoms are real and physical — anxiety produces genuine changes in the body. That does not mean something is being missed."
  • "We have checked for the things that matter. I am not going to keep repeating tests, because that feeds the worry rather than settling it."
  • "Avoiding what frightens you makes it stronger. Facing it gradually is what makes it shrink — that is what the therapy does."
  • "This medication takes longer to work for anxiety than for low mood — six to twelve weeks. The first week may feel more jittery; that settles."
  • "Cut the coffee and energy drinks right down. It sounds minor and it often is not."

⌖Local context

  • Somatic presentation is culturally normal here — patients may lack, or not use, a psychological vocabulary for distress. Meet them in the language they use.
  • Energy drink consumption is high in the region, particularly in young men, and is a frequent and reversible driver.
  • Ramadan and Hajj periods bring sleep disruption, crowding and schedule upheaval — anticipate symptom fluctuation and plan around it rather than escalating treatment.
  • Stigma makes patients more willing to accept a 'physical' explanation; the honest bridge is explaining the physiology of autonomic arousal, which is both true and acceptable.

Insomnia

Where the evidence and the prescription pad point in opposite directions

◍At a glance

  • What it is: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early morning waking, despite adequate opportunity, with daytime consequences.
  • Chronic insomnia disorder: ≥3 nights per week for ≥3 months.
  • How common: around a third of adults report insomnia symptoms; roughly 10% meet criteria for the disorder.
  • Why it matters: it is a bidirectional risk factor — insomnia both follows and predicts depression, and treating it independently improves mood outcomes.
🚩 Red flags
  • Excessive daytime somnolence with snoring or witnessed apneas — investigate for OSA before any sedative.
  • Cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations — possible narcolepsy.
  • Acting out dreams (REM sleep behaviour disorder) — may precede neurodegenerative disease; refer.
  • Parasomnia with risk of injury to self or a bed partner.
  • Insomnia with suicidal ideation — insomnia is an independent, modifiable suicide risk factor; treat it urgently.
  • Abrupt severe insomnia with agitation and reduced sleep need — consider mania.
Interactive

Insomnia Severity Index

Recall period: last 2 weeks. Seven items, 0–4 each; total 0–28.

◐Clinical features

  • Difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early and being unable to return to sleep.
  • Daytime consequences are required — fatigue, mood disturbance, impaired concentration, reduced performance, or daytime sleepiness. Someone who sleeps five hours and functions well is a short sleeper, not an insomniac.
  • Frequently accompanied by conditioned arousal — the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness, and the patient sleeps better away from home or on the sofa. This is diagnostically useful.
  • Sleep-related worry and clock-watching perpetuate the cycle.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis. Take a full sleep history: bedtime, latency, awakenings, final waking, rise time, naps, and what they do when they cannot sleep.
  • A one- to two-week sleep diary is more informative than recall and is the single most useful thing you can ask for.
  • ISI above for severity and tracking.
  • STOP-BANG where OSA is possible. Consider actigraphy or polysomnography only where a specific sleep disorder is suspected — not for uncomplicated insomnia.
  • Investigations: ferritin (restless legs), TSH, glucose. Otherwise directed by history.

⇄Differential diagnosis

✚Management

⚠ CBT-I is first-line — not medication, and not sleep hygiene alone

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, ahead of any drug, with effects that persist after treatment ends — precisely what hypnotics do not achieve.

  • Active components are stimulus control (bed for sleep only; leave the bed if awake >15–20 minutes) and sleep restriction (limit time in bed to actual sleep time, then extend). These do the work.
  • Sleep hygiene alone is not adequate treatment — handing over a hygiene leaflet is not CBT-I, and is often why patients believe non-drug approaches failed.
  • Where formal CBT-I is unavailable, the core behavioural components can be delivered across a few short primary care visits, or via validated digital programmes.
  • If medication is used: short-term only, lowest effective dose, alongside — not instead of — behavioural treatment, with the stop plan agreed before the first prescription.
  • Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs: tolerance, dependence, rebound insomnia, next-day impairment, driving risk. Avoid in the elderly — falls, fractures, cognitive impairment; on the Beers Criteria.
  • Melatonin: most useful for circadian problems and in older adults (prolonged-release); modest effect on latency in primary insomnia.
  • Sedating antidepressants at low dose are reasonable where depression coexists, less justified in isolated insomnia.
  • Never treat suspected OSA with a sedative.

◈Special situations

  • Older adults: sleep architecture changes normally with age — earlier timing and lighter sleep are not pathology. Avoid hypnotics; melatonin and CBT-I are preferred.
  • Pregnancy: behavioural approaches; avoid hypnotics. Consider restless legs, which is common and often iron-responsive.
  • Shift workers — very common in healthcare: this is circadian misalignment, managed with light timing, strategic napping and sleep scheduling rather than as insomnia.
  • Ramadan: sleep timing shifts markedly and daytime napping increases. This is expected, usually self-limiting, and should not be diagnosed as chronic insomnia — but caffeine intake after iftar frequently makes it much worse and is worth asking about specifically.

⊘Prevention

  • Treat acute insomnia early and behaviourally — most chronic insomnia begins as an acute stress-related episode that becomes conditioned.
  • Avoid initiating hypnotics for acute insomnia wherever possible; long-term dependence usually begins with a short course that was never stopped.
  • Maintain regular rise time, limit daytime napping, and protect the bed as a sleep-only cue.
  • Treat comorbid depression, anxiety, pain, and OSA — insomnia often does not resolve until these do.

⚠Complications

  • Incident depression — insomnia is an independent predictor, not merely a symptom.
  • Increased suicide risk, independent of depression severity.
  • Impaired occupational performance and increased accident and motor vehicle risk — directly relevant to clinicians and drivers.
  • Hypnotic dependence and rebound insomnia on withdrawal.
  • In older adults: falls, fractures, and cognitive impairment from sedatives.
  • Associations with hypertension and metabolic dysfunction.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care: uncomplicated chronic insomnia, with behavioural treatment.
  • Refer to sleep medicine: suspected OSA, narcolepsy, REM sleep behaviour disorder, injurious parasomnia, or refractory insomnia despite adequate CBT-I.
  • Refer to psychiatry: where insomnia is driven by an untreated mood, anxiety, or substance use disorder that is not responding.
  • Structured withdrawal support for established long-term hypnotic use.

↗Prognosis

  • CBT-I produces improvement in the majority, and the gains are maintained after treatment ends — the key advantage over medication.
  • Untreated chronic insomnia tends to persist for years and is unlikely to remit spontaneously once conditioned.
  • Hypnotic-treated insomnia typically relapses on discontinuation, because the underlying conditioning is untreated.
  • Prognosis is worse where an untreated comorbidity — depression, OSA, pain — is driving it.

↻Follow-up

  • 2–4 weeks after starting behavioural treatment, reviewing the sleep diary — sleep restriction transiently worsens daytime sleepiness before it improves, and patients abandon it without warning.
  • Monthly until stable, tracking with the ISI.
  • If a hypnotic was started: review by the agreed stop date, without exception — unreviewed repeat prescriptions are how dependence happens.
  • Re-screen for depression and OSA if insomnia is not improving as expected.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "The bed should be for sleep only. If you are awake more than about twenty minutes, get up, go somewhere dim and quiet, and come back when sleepy."
  • "Keep the same wake-up time every day, including weekends — that anchor matters more than bedtime."
  • "Do not chase lost sleep with naps or early nights; it makes tomorrow night worse."
  • "Sleeping tablets work for a few weeks and then stop working, and coming off them causes a rebound — that is why we are not starting there."
  • "This will get worse for a week or two before it gets better. That is how the treatment works, not a sign it is failing."

Somatic Symptom Presentations

The consultations that feel unsatisfying to both sides — and are entirely manageable

◍At a glance

  • What it is: one or more distressing somatic symptoms plus disproportionate and persistent thoughts, anxiety, or time and energy devoted to them, typically >6 months.
  • The crucial modern change: the diagnosis no longer requires the symptom to be medically unexplained. A patient with genuine diabetes or genuine back pain can have somatic symptom disorder — what defines it is the disproportionate psychological response, not absent pathology.
  • Why that matters: it removes the adversarial 'is it real or not' framing. The symptom is real. The suffering is real. The question is what maintains it.
  • How common: a substantial minority of primary care attenders; among the highest healthcare utilisers.
🚩 Red flags
  • New, changed, or objectively abnormal findings — these patients develop appendicitis and myocardial infarction like everyone else. Every new symptom deserves fresh assessment on its merits.
  • Unintentional weight loss, night sweats, fever, or lymphadenopathy.
  • Focal neurological deficit, or symptoms with a clear anatomical pattern.
  • Suicidal ideation — the distress here is genuine and the risk is real.
  • Escalating opioid or benzodiazepine use.
  • Symptoms beginning after age 50 without prior history.

◐Clinical features

  • Persistent physical symptoms — commonly pain, fatigue, gastrointestinal or neurological — accompanied by high health anxiety.
  • Disproportionate concern about the seriousness of symptoms; persistent high anxiety about health; excessive time and energy devoted to symptoms.
  • Repeated consultations, often with multiple clinicians; a thick file of normal investigations.
  • Brief reassurance after each normal result, followed by return of anxiety — the reassurance cycle.
  • Related presentations: illness anxiety disorder (fear of having a disease, minimal symptoms); functional neurological disorder (neurological symptoms with positive incompatibility signs — a rule-in diagnosis, not one of exclusion).

⊕Diagnosis

  • Positive clinical diagnosis based on the pattern of symptoms plus the disproportionate response — not a diagnosis of exclusion.
  • Review the existing record thoroughly before ordering anything: much of the workup has usually been done, sometimes repeatedly.
  • Decide what genuinely needs exclusion, do it once, deliberately, and say clearly that it is complete.
  • Screen actively for depression and anxiety — PHQ-9 and GAD-7. They are present in a large proportion and are the most treatable component.
  • Ask what the patient believes is happening and what they fear (ICEE) — this is the most informative part of the consultation, not a formality.

✚Management

  • One consistent clinician. Fragmented care drives repeat investigation and contradictory explanations. Continuity is itself therapeutic.
  • Regularly scheduled brief visits, not symptom-triggered ones. This is the single most effective structural change — if appointments are earned by symptoms, symptoms are reinforced. Book the next visit at a fixed interval regardless of how they feel.
  • Investigate finitely. Each additional low-yield test increases health anxiety rather than reducing it, and incidental findings generate new problems.
  • Examine the patient at each visit. It communicates being taken seriously in a way words do not, and it detects genuine new pathology.
  • Validate without endorsing a false mechanism: "I believe your pain is real and I can see how disabling it is. I don't think it's cancer, and here's why. What I do think is happening is…"
  • Shift the goal from cure to function — targets in terms of activity, work, sleep and social life rather than symptom eradication.
  • CBT has the best evidence. Graded activity and physiotherapy help where deconditioning has set in. Treat comorbid depression and anxiety actively.
⚠ What makes it worse
  • "There is nothing wrong" or "all the tests are normal" and stopping there — heard as disbelief.
  • One more test to reassure. It reassures briefly and raises the threshold for the next reassurance.
  • Onward referral for the same symptom without a clear question.
  • Opioids or benzodiazepines for chronic somatic distress.
  • Abrupt confrontation with a psychological explanation the patient has not been prepared for — this usually ends the relationship.
  • Assuming everything is somatic once the label is applied — the most dangerous error in this topic.

◈Special situations

  • Children and adolescents: recurrent abdominal pain and headache are common; involve parents, avoid excessive investigation, and address school avoidance early.
  • Older adults: higher pre-test probability of organic disease — the threshold for investigation should be higher, not lower.
  • Comorbid chronic disease: having diabetes or a genuine chronic condition does not exclude the diagnosis; it makes it harder and more important to get right.
  • Migrant and pilgrim populations: language and cultural distance amplify both the somatic idiom and the risk of the clinician mis-reading it.

⊘Prevention

  • Prevent iatrogenic entrenchment — this is where prevention actually operates. Avoid unnecessary tests, unclear explanations, and referral chains at the first presentation.
  • Explain normal results properly at the time: what was tested, what it rules out, and what the plan is now.
  • Early identification and treatment of anxiety and depression prevents progression to entrenched somatic presentations.
  • Establish continuity early — a named clinician from the outset prevents the fragmented pattern developing.
  • In children, avoid reinforcing illness behaviour and support prompt return to school and activity.

⚠Complications

  • Iatrogenic harm — unnecessary procedures, radiation exposure, incidental findings, and surgical complications from operations that were never indicated.
  • Opioid and benzodiazepine dependence.
  • Occupational disability and social withdrawal; substantial economic cost to the patient.
  • Breakdown of the therapeutic relationship, and 'doctor shopping' with further fragmentation.
  • Missed organic disease — from both directions: over-investigation causing distraction, and premature closure once the label is applied.
  • Comorbid depression, anxiety and suicide risk.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care — this is fundamentally a primary care condition and continuity is the treatment. Onward referral for the same symptom is usually counterproductive.
  • Refer to psychology/psychiatry: where CBT is available and the patient is willing — refer early rather than after years of investigation; also for severe comorbid depression, anxiety, or suicidal ideation.
  • Refer to the relevant specialty only with a specific clinical question, and ideally with a conversation rather than a blind letter.
  • Neurology for suspected functional neurological disorder — ideally to a clinician who will explain the positive diagnosis rather than say 'nothing is wrong'.
  • Admission is almost never indicated and frequently harmful.

↗Prognosis

  • Variable, and substantially better with a structured approach — continuity, scheduled visits, limited investigation, and treatment of comorbid mood disorder all improve outcome.
  • Untreated, the course tends to be chronic and fluctuating, with progressive disability and healthcare utilisation.
  • Better prognosis with: shorter duration before structured care, fewer prior investigations, treatable comorbid depression or anxiety, and absence of ongoing litigation or benefit dispute.
  • Full symptom resolution is an unrealistic target for most; functional improvement is achievable in the majority and is the right goal to set.

↻Follow-up

  • Fixed-interval appointments — typically every 4–6 weeks initially, then extending. Booked in advance, independent of symptoms. This is the core intervention.
  • Brief, structured visits with a physical examination each time.
  • Review function (work, activity, sleep, social contact) as the outcome measure rather than symptom count.
  • Re-screen mood at intervals.
  • Handover explicitly if you are away — an unfamiliar clinician meeting this presentation cold usually restarts the investigation cycle.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "I believe your symptoms are real. I am not saying this is in your head."
  • "The tests we have done rule out X and Y, and I am confident about that. Repeating them would not tell us anything new, and it tends to make the worry worse rather than better."
  • "Bodies produce real physical symptoms under stress — muscle tension, gut changes, heightened pain sensitivity. That is a mechanism, not imagination."
  • "We are going to meet regularly, whether you are having a bad patch or not, so you do not have to be unwell to be seen."
  • "The aim is to get you doing more of what matters to you, even while some symptoms are still there."

⌖Local context

◐ Somatic idioms of distress — read this before labelling

Across much of the Arab world and South Asia, expressing psychological distress through the body is a normal cultural idiom, not a disorder in itself. Chest tightness, heat in the body, heaviness, or generalised pain are legitimate and communicable ways of expressing emotional distress — particularly where a psychiatric label carries stigma.

  • Do not reflexively diagnose somatic symptom disorder in a patient who is simply expressing distress somatically. Many are describing depression or anxiety in the vocabulary available to them.
  • Screen for depression and anxiety in every such presentation.
  • In a Medina practice you are also seeing pilgrims and residents from many cultures. Ask what the patient believes is happening in their body and what they fear — usually the most useful question available.
  • ICEE is not an exam formality here; it is the actual clinical tool for this presentation.

Suicide Risk Assessment

A core primary care competency — most people who die by suicide have seen a doctor recently

◍At a glance

If you are reading this about yourself

This section is written for clinicians. If you have come here because of your own thoughts — please reach out today rather than reading on. In Saudi Arabia the National Center for Mental Health Promotion counselling line is 920033360; in an emergency call 997 (Saudi Red Crescent) or 911 where the unified service operates. You can go to any emergency department, or tell someone you trust right now. These feelings are treatable, and they do change.

  • What this is: a structured clinical process — asking directly, weighing static and modifiable factors, and producing a concrete safety plan. It is not a score.
  • Why it sits in primary care: a large proportion of people who die by suicide have contact with a primary care clinician in the preceding weeks to months. This is the highest-leverage point in the system.
  • The central skill: asking plainly and tolerating the answer.
🚩 Red flags
  • Active ideation with intent, a plan, or preparatory acts — do not let the patient leave unaccompanied.
  • A recent attempt of any apparent medical severity — medical lethality correlates poorly with future risk.
  • Psychotic symptoms, especially command hallucinations.
  • Severe agitation, or acute intoxication with expressed ideation.
  • Profound hopelessness, or a sudden unexplained calm after a period of distress.
  • No safety plan achievable, or no available support at home.
  • Your own clinical unease — a legitimate reason to escalate. Discuss with a psychiatric colleague rather than deciding alone.

◐Clinical features

  • Ask directly. Asking about suicide does not plant the idea or increase risk — this is the most persistent and most harmful myth in the area, and for many patients being asked is a relief.
  • Euphemism invites a euphemistic answer: "Have you had thoughts of ending your life?" not "You're not thinking of doing anything silly?"
  • Move from passive to active: wishing to be dead or not to wake → active thoughts of killing oneself → whether they have thought about how → intent and preparation.
  • Ask about timeframe — now, past month, ever — and about any previous attempt at any point in life.
  • Ask about hopelessness specifically; it carries more weight than sadness.
  • Document what you asked and what the patient said, in their own words.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Suicide risk is a clinical formulation, not a diagnosis or a score.
  • Structured tools help organise the conversation: the Columbia Protocol (C-SSRS) moves through exactly the passive-to-active sequence above and is the most widely used.
  • PHQ-9 item 9 is a screen, not an assessment — a positive response mandates a full conversation regardless of the total score.
  • Risk scores do not predict individual outcomes. No instrument reliably stratifies who will act. Their value is structuring the interview and identifying what is modifiable.
  • Assess the underlying disorder in parallel — depression, substance use, psychosis, personality disorder, and physical illness all change the plan.

  • Follow-up must be active and soon — days, not weeks, with a named person responsible.

◈Special situations

◐ Groups needing particular attention here
  • Expatriate workers — documented higher risk, compounded by financial strain, family separation, language barriers and limited access to care. Ask about isolation and pressures at home they may feel unable to discuss.
  • Adolescents and young adults — academic pressure and bullying including online. National Family Safety Program: 800 122 2777, Sun–Thu 8am–10pm.
  • Older adults — ideation is often not volunteered and gets attributed to physical illness or normal ageing. Ask anyway; completion rates in this group are high.
  • Perinatal women — screen explicitly; suicide is a leading cause of maternal death in the year after birth.
  • Chronic pain and serious physical illness — under-recognised risk group in general practice.
  • Stigma is a dominant barrier to disclosure in this region. Explicit early reassurance about what will and will not be shared makes disclosure substantially more likely — be honest about the limits of confidentiality where safety is at stake rather than promising absolute secrecy.

⊘Prevention

  • Treat the underlying disorder — effective treatment of depression, substance use and psychosis is the largest single contributor to risk reduction.
  • Treat insomnia and agitation actively — both are modifiable, independent risk factors.
  • Means safety counselling for at-risk patients, as above.
  • Brief contact interventions work — a phone call, message or letter after a crisis or discharge measurably reduces subsequent risk. This is cheap and underused.
  • Continuity of care and a reliable therapeutic relationship are themselves protective.
  • Restrict quantities and consider dispensing intervals when prescribing to a patient at risk.
  • At population level: responsible media reporting, and reducing access to common means.

⚠Complications

  • Death.
  • Serious physical injury and long-term disability following an attempt.
  • Profound effect on family — bereavement by suicide carries elevated risk of complicated grief, depression, and suicide in survivors. Offer follow-up to the family.
  • Effect on the clinical team — clinician distress after patient suicide is real and under-supported; seek supervision and peer support.
  • Repeat attempts: risk is highest in the days to weeks after an index attempt and after discharge from hospital.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care with a safety plan: passive ideation without intent or plan, in a patient with support, engagement, and reliable early follow-up.
  • Same-day psychiatric assessment: active ideation with intent or plan; recent attempt; psychosis; severe agitation; inadequate support; or clinician concern.
  • Emergency department / admission: imminent risk, inability to agree or engage with a safety plan, intoxication with ideation, or medical consequences of an attempt.
  • Do not send an at-risk patient home alone or unaccompanied. Arrange transport and handover to a named person.
  • Document the assessment, the reasoning, the plan agreed, who was involved, and the follow-up arranged.

↗Prognosis

  • Suicidal crises are typically time-limited and highly ambivalent — which is exactly why buying time works and why means restriction is effective.
  • Most people who survive an attempt do not go on to die by suicide, and the majority who receive treatment for the underlying disorder improve. This is worth saying to patients and families.
  • However, risk is markedly elevated in the days to weeks after an attempt and after psychiatric discharge — these are the highest-risk transition points and warrant active contact.
  • Long-term risk remains elevated after any attempt, which is why previous attempt is the strongest predictor.

↻Follow-up

  • Within days — not weeks — after any assessment identifying risk. Book it before the patient leaves.
  • Active contact if the patient does not attend: call them. Non-attendance in this group is a clinical event, not an administrative one.
  • Brief contact after discharge from emergency or inpatient care measurably reduces risk — a call or message is worthwhile.
  • Review and update the safety plan at each contact; it is a living document, not a form.
  • Track the underlying disorder with PHQ-9 or equivalent, and re-ask about ideation each time.
  • Involve family with consent, and give them a clear route to make contact.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "Thank you for telling me. That took courage, and it was the right thing to do."
  • "These thoughts are a symptom of an illness that is treatable — they are not a character flaw and they are not permanent."
  • "Feeling like this can change, often faster than people expect once we start treating what is underneath."
  • "We are going to write down a plan together for what you do if it gets bad, so you are not deciding in the moment."
  • "I would like your brother/wife to help hold onto some things for a while — not because you cannot be trusted, but because these moments pass and we want time on your side."
  • "Here is exactly who to call and when. You do not need to be at the worst point to use it."

Borderline Personality Disorder

The diagnosis most damaged by stigma — and one with a genuinely good prognosis

◍At a glance

  • What it is: a pervasive pattern of instability in emotion regulation, self-image, and relationships, with marked impulsivity, beginning by early adulthood and present across contexts.
  • The core deficit is emotion regulation — emotions rise faster, peak higher, and return to baseline more slowly than in others. Almost every criterion is downstream of that.
  • How common: around 1–2% of the general population, roughly 6% of primary care attenders, and far higher in psychiatric and emergency settings.
  • Why it matters in primary care: these patients are frequent attenders, often carry a long list of other diagnoses, and are among the most likely to receive poor care because of clinician frustration. Recognising the pattern changes the whole approach.

  • The single most important fact in this topic: BPD has a good prognosis. Longitudinal studies show the large majority achieve symptomatic remission over 10 years, and remission is often stable. Therapeutic pessimism about this diagnosis is not evidence-based — and communicating that is itself therapeutic.

🚩 Red flags
  • Acute-on-chronic escalation of suicide risk — a change from this patient's baseline is what matters, not the presence of chronic ideation. Know the baseline so you can recognise the change.
  • Self-harm with serious medical consequence, or of escalating severity.
  • New or worsening comorbid major depression — the commonest driver of acute risk in BPD.
  • Escalating substance use, or new intoxication at presentation.
  • Psychotic symptoms beyond transient, stress-related, brief episodes.
  • Sudden loss of a key relationship, therapist, or support — an abandonment trigger is a high-risk period.
  • Pregnancy, postpartum, or a child protection concern.
  • Complete disengagement from care after a period of engagement.

◐Clinical features

  • DSM-5: ≥5 of 9 criteria, pervasive and beginning by early adulthood:
  • #CriterionHow it looks in clinic

  • Comorbidity is the rule, not the exception: depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use, eating disorders, ADHD. Untreated comorbidity is usually what brings the patient in.
  • Trauma history is common — childhood adversity, neglect, or abuse — but is neither universal nor required for the diagnosis.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis from a longitudinal pattern across contexts — not from a single distressed presentation. A person in acute crisis can look like this without having BPD.
  • Collateral history and the pattern over years are more informative than any cross-sectional assessment.
  • Screening instruments (e.g. the McLean screening instrument) exist but are for case-finding, not diagnosis; formal diagnosis is usually psychiatric.
  • Investigations: none diagnostic. Exclude organic contributors and check thyroid function; toxicology where substance use is possible; screen for comorbid depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), PTSD and substance use.
  • ⚠ Diagnosing in adolescents

    BPD can be validly diagnosed before age 18, and both NICE and Australian guidance support this where the pattern is pervasive, persistent and unlikely to be a developmental phase. Withholding the diagnosis until adulthood delays access to effective treatment during the period when it works best. The old reluctance was about stigma, not evidence — but the diagnosis must be made carefully and explained well, not applied to any distressed adolescent.

✚Management

⚠ Structured psychotherapy is the treatment. Medication is not.

No medication is indicated for the core features of BPD, and guidance is explicit that drugs should not be used to treat BPD itself or its individual symptoms. Polypharmacy in this group is common, harmful, and hard to unwind.

  • First-line: structured, evidence-based psychotherapy — Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT), Schema-Focused Therapy, Transference-Focused Psychotherapy, or STEPPS. All outperform unstructured support.
  • Good Psychiatric Management (GPM) is designed to be delivered by non-specialists and is the most realistic framework for primary care: psychoeducation, a focus on life outside treatment, clear expectations, and a case-management stance. If you learn one approach, learn this one.
  • Medication has a limited, adjunctive role only: treat genuine comorbid conditions (major depression, PTSD, ADHD, bipolar) on their own merits. Short-term antipsychotic may modestly help acute cognitive-perceptual symptoms or severe anger. Review and deprescribe regularly.
  • Avoid benzodiazepines — disinhibition, dependence, and elevated risk in overdose. This group is particularly vulnerable to all three.
  • Be cautious with quantities dispensed in patients with a history of overdose.
  • 🔑 The primary care stance — this is where you actually help
    • Consistency beats intensity. Regular, predictable, time-limited appointments — booked in advance, not crisis-triggered. Same principle as somatic presentations, and for the same reason.
    • Clear, kind, explicit boundaries, set early and calmly, and applied consistently. Boundaries are containing, not rejecting — and inconsistency is what destabilises.
    • Name the plan for crises in advance, when everyone is calm: what the patient does, who they call, what you will and will not do.
    • Validate the emotion without necessarily endorsing the interpretation — "I can see why that felt like being abandoned" is not the same as agreeing that it was.
    • Manage your own reaction. Strong feelings — protectiveness, irritation, being special, wanting to discharge — are diagnostic information, not failures. Notice them and discuss with a colleague.
    • Protect the team from splitting — communicate, document, and agree a shared approach so that different staff are not giving different answers.
    • Distinguish chronic from acute risk (below). This is the single most useful clinical distinction in managing BPD.

◈Special situations

  • Chronic vs acute suicide risk — the central management distinction. Chronic ideation and self-harm are part of the baseline presentation. Hospitalisation for chronic risk is not protective and can be counterproductive — it can reinforce crisis-driven care and cause deterioration. What warrants escalation is an acute change from that baseline: new intent, new plan, a major loss, worsening depression, or escalating substance use. Establish and document the baseline explicitly so a change is recognisable — by you and by whoever sees them at 2am.
  • Self-harm without suicidal intent is common and typically serves emotion regulation. Treat the injury without judgement, address the function, and avoid both alarm and dismissal. Never respond punitively.
  • Pregnancy and postpartum: elevated risk period; strengthen support and monitoring, involve maternity and mental health services early.
  • Comorbid substance use must be addressed in parallel — it markedly amplifies impulsivity and risk.
  • Physical health: this group has high rates of smoking, obesity, and chronic pain, and their physical complaints are frequently dismissed once the diagnosis is known. Guard against that.
  • Adolescents: early intervention works well; involve family; avoid the diagnosis becoming a barrier to care.

⊘Prevention

  • Primary: reducing childhood adversity, maltreatment and neglect; supporting secure attachment and parenting. Largely a public health rather than clinical lever, but real.
  • Secondary — the achievable one: early recognition in adolescence and early adulthood, and prompt access to structured psychotherapy, substantially improves the trajectory. Delayed diagnosis costs years.
  • Tertiary — mostly about preventing iatrogenic harm: avoid polypharmacy, avoid repeated brief admissions for chronic risk, avoid abrupt discharge from services, and avoid the rotating-clinician pattern that guarantees instability.
  • Prevent crises structurally: predictable appointments, an agreed crisis plan, and continuity of clinician.
  • Treat comorbid depression, PTSD and substance use — these drive most acute deteriorations.

⚠Complications

  • Suicide — historically cited at around 8–10% in clinical cohorts, though contemporary treated cohorts appear substantially lower. Risk is highest early in the course and with comorbid depression or substance use.
  • Repeated self-harm with cumulative physical consequences — scarring, tendon and nerve injury, infection.
  • Substance use disorder.
  • Iatrogenic harm: polypharmacy, dependence on prescribed sedatives, repeated unhelpful admissions, and the consequences of being labelled 'difficult' — including dismissal of genuine physical illness.
  • Occupational instability, relationship breakdown, financial and legal difficulty, housing instability.
  • Interpersonal consequences for children and family; consider parenting support and safeguarding where relevant.
  • Higher rates of physical morbidity and premature mortality from non-suicide causes.

→Disposition & referral

  • Manage in primary care: ongoing physical health care, continuity, comorbidity treatment, crisis-plan adherence, and coordination — with psychiatric input available. Most of these patients' contacts will be with you.
  • Refer to psychiatry / psychology: for diagnostic clarification, and to access structured psychotherapy (DBT, MBT and equivalents) — the definitive treatment. Refer early rather than after years.
  • Urgent psychiatric assessment: acute-on-chronic risk escalation, new intent or plan, psychosis, severe comorbid depression, or serious self-harm.
  • Admission: brief and purposeful only, for acute risk that cannot be managed otherwise. Prolonged admission for chronic risk is generally harmful — agree the purpose and the discharge criteria at the point of admission.
  • Do not discharge from your list because the patient is difficult. Loss of continuity is destabilising and predictably worsens outcomes; escalate and get support instead.

↗Prognosis

  • Genuinely good, and consistently better than clinicians expect. Prospective longitudinal studies show that the large majority achieve symptomatic remission over 10 years, and that remission is frequently sustained with low relapse rates.
  • Impulsivity and self-harm improve earliest and most reliably; chronic emptiness and interpersonal difficulties are the most persistent.
  • Functional recovery lags well behind symptomatic remission — work and relationship functioning improve more slowly, and this gap is the main remaining treatment target.
  • Structured psychotherapy accelerates the trajectory; it does not create a recovery that would not otherwise occur, but it substantially shortens the time and reduces the harm along the way.
  • Worse prognosis with: comorbid substance use disorder, severe childhood trauma, persistent major depression, and ongoing psychosocial instability.
  • Tell the patient this. Many have been told, or have read, that this is a permanent, untreatable condition. It is neither, and hearing otherwise from a clinician changes engagement.

↻Follow-up

  • Regular, predictable, pre-booked appointments — typically every 2–4 weeks initially, extending as stability improves. Fixed interval, not crisis-triggered: this is the core structural intervention.
  • Keep visits time-limited and structured; agree the agenda at the start.
  • Review the crisis plan at each visit and update it — it is a living document.
  • Re-establish the risk baseline periodically and document it, so that a change is recognisable to any clinician who sees them.
  • Review medication at every visit with a deprescribing intent — polypharmacy accumulates silently in this group.
  • Attend to physical health actively: screening, smoking, weight, contraception, and any physical complaint on its merits.
  • Plan explicitly for your own absences — leave, rotation, or transfer. Unannounced discontinuity is an abandonment trigger; flag it well in advance and name the covering clinician.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "This is a condition of emotion regulation — your emotions come faster and stronger and take longer to settle. That is not a character flaw, and it is not you being difficult."
  • "The most important thing I can tell you is that this gets better. Most people with this diagnosis improve substantially over the years, and many no longer meet criteria at all."
  • "The treatment that works is a specific kind of therapy, not tablets. Medication can help other things you also have, but it does not treat this directly — that is why I am careful about adding more."
  • "We are going to meet at regular times, whether things are good or bad. You do not have to be in crisis to be seen."
  • "Let's write down now what you do when things get bad, while we are both calm."
  • "When I am away, [named clinician] will be here and will know your plan. I will tell you before it happens."

⌖Local context

  • Access to DBT and MBT is limited across much of the region. Good Psychiatric Management is deliverable by a trained generalist and is the pragmatic answer where specialist therapy is unavailable — worth pursuing as a skill.
  • Stigma is compounded here — both the general stigma of mental illness and the specific stigma attached to self-harm. Patients and families may frame difficulties in moral or religious terms; respond without reinforcing shame.
  • Family involvement is usually valuable and often expected, but the relational instability of BPD frequently centres on the family. Obtain consent, be even-handed, and consider family psychoeducation.
  • Self-harm may be concealed with particular care for social and religious reasons — ask directly, privately, and without alarm.
  • Where a patient has sought spiritual healing, engage respectfully; the concern is delay in accessing effective treatment, not the practice itself.

Schizophrenia & Psychosis

Recognition is the primary care job — and so is the physical health that determines how long these patients live

◍At a glance

  • What it is: a disorder of psychosis with positive, negative and cognitive symptom domains, causing sustained functional impairment.
  • How common: lifetime prevalence roughly 0.3–0.7%. Onset typically late teens to early thirties, earlier in men.
  • Why primary care matters twice: first for early recognition — duration of untreated psychosis predicts outcome; and second for physical health, which is where these patients actually die 15–20 years early.
  • The prodrome often precedes frank psychosis by months to years — social withdrawal, falling function, odd beliefs, suspiciousness, deteriorating self-care in a young person.
🚩 Red flags
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome — fever, rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, raised CK. Stop the antipsychotic, emergency transfer.
  • Acute dystonia — oculogyric crisis, torticollis, laryngospasm. Distressing but treatable; urgent anticholinergic.
  • Clozapine patients: fever or infection (check neutrophils urgently), chest pain or dyspnea (myocarditis), or absent bowel movements (ileus — a genuine cause of death).
  • Catatonia — mutism, immobility, posturing, refusal of food and fluid. Medical emergency.
  • Command hallucinations, or risk of harm to self or others.
  • Suicidal ideation — substantially elevated risk, especially early in the illness and in those with preserved insight.
  • First-episode psychosis with fever, confusion, fluctuating consciousness, or focal signs — think delirium or encephalitis, not schizophrenia.

◐Clinical features

  • Positive symptoms: delusions, hallucinations (auditory most common), disorganised speech, grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour.
  • Negative symptoms: diminished emotional expression, avolition, alogia, anhedonia, asociality. Often mistaken for depression or laziness, respond least well to medication, and drive most long-term disability.
  • Cognitive symptoms: impaired attention, working memory and executive function — frequently the biggest barrier to work and study.
  • DSM-5: ≥2 symptoms for a significant portion of one month, at least one being delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech, with continuous disturbance ≥6 months including prodromal or residual phases.

⊕Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis, usually confirmed by psychiatry — but the workup that rules out organic causes belongs in primary care and is where you add most value.
  • Baseline investigations at first presentation: CBC, electrolytes, renal and liver function, glucose/HbA1c, calcium, TSH, B12, urine toxicology; consider HIV and syphilis serology.
  • Neuroimaging where there are focal signs, atypical features, late onset, or poor treatment response.
  • Baseline metabolic panel before antipsychotics: weight, BMI, waist circumference, BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids, and ECG where cardiac risk or a QT-prolonging agent is planned.
  • ⚠ First-episode psychosis needs an organic workup

    Do not assume a primary psychiatric cause on first presentation.

✚Management

  • Refer urgently — do not observe and wait. Longer duration of untreated psychosis is associated with worse symptomatic and functional recovery, and it is one of the few prognostic factors that can actually be influenced. Primary care controls most of it.
  • Antipsychotics are usually specialist-initiated; do not start before assessment unless local protocol directs otherwise.
  • Ongoing monitoring is shared, and much of it falls to family medicine — see the follow-up section.
  • Support adherence: ask about side effects specifically (sedation, weight, sexual dysfunction, akathisia) — these drive discontinuation and patients often do not volunteer them. Long-acting injectables are an option where adherence is a barrier.
  • Psychosocial: family psychoeducation reduces relapse; supported employment and education improve function; CBT for psychosis has evidence for persistent symptoms.
  • Smoking cessation — rates are very high; offer support actively and repeatedly. Note that smoking induces clozapine and olanzapine metabolism, so stopping can raise levels significantly.
  • ⚠ The mortality gap is cardiovascular, not suicide

    People with schizophrenia die roughly 15–20 years earlier than the general population, and most of that excess is cardiovascular and metabolic disease — though suicide risk is also substantially elevated. Much of it is preventable and sits squarely in primary care.

    • Second-generation antipsychotics cause substantial weight gain, dyslipidemia and diabetes — clozapine and olanzapine most; aripiprazole, ziprasidone and lurasidone least.
    • These patients are systematically under-screened and under-treated for ordinary physical illness, because complaints get attributed to the psychiatric diagnosis. This is called diagnostic overshadowing and it kills people.
    • Treat their hypertension, diabetes and lipids to the same standard as anyone else's. This is the single highest-value thing family medicine does for this group.

◈Special situations

  • Pregnancy: do not stop antipsychotics abruptly — relapse risk is high and carries its own danger. Coordinate with psychiatry and obstetrics; screen for gestational diabetes given metabolic effects.
  • Clozapine: mandatory neutrophil monitoring; also watch for myocarditis (early, often first weeks — chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia, fever) and severe constipation progressing to ileus. Ask about bowels routinely and treat constipation aggressively.
  • Older adults: increased mortality with antipsychotics in dementia-related psychosis; higher sensitivity to EPS and anticholinergic burden.
  • Ramadan: fasting affects dosing schedules, hydration and sleep; clozapine levels and lithium in particular need review. Plan before the month, and note that dehydration raises toxicity risk.
  • Comorbid substance use — especially cannabis and stimulants — worsens course and relapse risk; address directly.

⊘Prevention

  • Primary prevention is limited, but reducing adolescent cannabis exposure is the most actionable modifiable population risk factor.
  • Secondary — the high-yield one: shortening duration of untreated psychosis through early recognition and rapid referral. Early intervention services improve outcomes.
  • Relapse prevention: adherence support, long-acting injectables where appropriate, family psychoeducation, and early identification of relapse signatures (each patient tends to relapse in a recognisable pattern — document theirs).
  • Preventing the physical mortality gap: metabolic monitoring, cardiovascular risk treatment, smoking cessation, cancer screening. This is the largest preventable harm in the condition.
  • Preventing suicide — highest risk early in the illness, after discharge, and in those with preserved insight and depression.

⚠Complications

  • Cardiometabolic disease — the leading cause of premature death: obesity, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease.
  • Suicide — substantially elevated lifetime risk, highest early in the illness.
  • Movement disorders — acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, and tardive dyskinesia (often irreversible; examine for it, do not just ask).
  • Hyperprolactinemia — galactorrhea, amenorrhea, sexual dysfunction, reduced bone density.
  • Clozapine-specific: agranulocytosis, myocarditis, ileus, seizures, myocardial and metabolic effects.
  • Cognitive impairment and negative symptoms driving unemployment, poverty, housing instability and social isolation.
  • Substance use disorder; victimisation — people with schizophrenia are far more often victims than perpetrators of violence.

→Disposition & referral

  • Refer urgently to psychiatry or early intervention for any suspected first-episode psychosis.
  • Emergency assessment / admission: risk to self or others, command hallucinations, catatonia, inability to maintain nutrition or safety, NMS, or clozapine red flags.
  • Shared care in primary care: physical health monitoring, metabolic and cardiovascular management, vaccination, cancer screening, contraception, and treatment of any intercurrent illness — with psychiatry retaining medication oversight.
  • Act on disengagement rather than discharging — non-attendance in this group is a clinical event. Contact the patient, the family, and the mental health team.

↗Prognosis

  • Heterogeneous. A minority have a single episode with good recovery; many have a relapsing–remitting course with variable residual symptoms; a proportion have persistent illness with significant disability.
  • Better prognosis: later onset, acute onset, prominent mood symptoms, good premorbid function, female sex, short duration of untreated psychosis, good treatment response and adherence, and strong family support.
  • Worse prognosis: insidious onset, prominent negative and cognitive symptoms, poor premorbid function, long duration of untreated psychosis, ongoing substance use, and social isolation.
  • Negative and cognitive symptoms are the main determinants of functional outcome — more than positive symptoms, which respond best to medication.
  • Life expectancy is reduced by roughly 15–20 years, mostly cardiovascular — and that part is the most modifiable element of the prognosis, by you.

💬What to tell the patient

  • "This is a treatable medical condition affecting how the brain processes information. It is not a punishment, a weakness, or possession."
  • "Starting treatment early makes a real difference to how well things go — that is why I do not want to wait and see."
  • "The medication has side effects and I want you to tell me about them rather than stopping — most of them we can do something about."
  • "Looking after your heart, your weight and your sugar matters as much as the psychiatric treatment. That part is my job and I will keep on at it."
  • "Let's write down the early signs that things are slipping, so you and your family can spot them before it becomes a crisis."

⌖Local context

◐ Distinguishing psychosis from normative religious and cultural experience

This requires care and is easy to get wrong in both directions — pathologising genuine faith, or dismissing genuine illness as religiosity.

  • Ask whether the belief or experience is shared and endorsed by the patient's own religious or cultural community. A belief held in common with that community, in a culturally recognisable form, is not a delusion — this is explicit in DSM-5.
  • Ask a knowledgeable family member or, with consent, a community figure whether what the patient describes falls within normal practice for them.
  • Look at function and form rather than content. Deteriorating self-care, withdrawal, disorganisation and distress point to illness regardless of the religious content of the symptoms.
  • Delayed presentation is common where families seek spiritual healing first, often for entirely coherent reasons within their framework. Respond without dismissiveness — the aim is to add medical assessment alongside, not to demand the family abandon what they trust. Dismissing it outright usually loses the family entirely. See Traditional & Complementary Medicine.
  • The practical concern is duration of untreated psychosis, not the family's beliefs.
