Adult & adolescent · chronic management

Adult Asthma

The Saudi Initiative for Asthma is the national standard, and its defining feature is practical: it uses the ACT score itself to decide where to start treatment. This guide follows SINA 2024 as the primary framework, with GINA 2026 layered in where it adds or diverges.

SINA 2024 — primary GINA 2026 — overlay Ages 12+
Diagnosis ACT & control Where to start The 5 steps GINA tracks Not controlled? New in GINA 2026 Referral Patient tools

Diagnosis

Clinical history and examination, supported by spirometry with reversibility. No single test confirms asthma — and a normal spirometry does not exclude it.

1

History — the pattern matters more than any single symptom

  • Wheeze, cough, shortness of breath, chest tightness — none specific alone, but the combination raises probability substantially.
  • Variable over time; patient may be entirely asymptomatic between attacks. Typically worse at night or early morning.
  • Ask specifically: personal or family history of asthma, eczema, allergic rhinitis; recurrent wheeze; night cough; cough or wheeze after exercise.
  • Do colds "go to the chest" or take more than 10 days to clear?
  • Worsening after aspirin/NSAIDs or beta-blockers?
  • Any features suggesting occupational asthma?
Local trigger note

SINA explicitly lists bukhoor (burning incense) alongside pollens, dust, animal dander, and cigarette smoke as an environmental trigger to ask about — a question easy to omit if you're working from an imported guideline. Worth asking routinely here, along with shisha exposure.

2

Examination

  • Chest may be entirely normal in stable, controlled asthma — a normal exam proves nothing.
  • Characteristic finding: bilateral, widespread, high-pitched, variable expiratory wheeze.
  • Wheeze indicates airway narrowing but does not correlate with severity.
  • Examine the upper airway: nasal mucosal swelling, polyps, postnasal drip. Look for atopic dermatitis.
Findings that point away from asthma — reconsider the diagnosis
  • Localized (rather than widespread) wheeze
  • Crackles, stridor, clubbing, or heart murmurs
3

Confirming with objective testing

TestSupportive resultCaveat
Spirometry with reversibilityFEV₁ improvement ≥12% AND ≥200 mL post-bronchodilatorNormal spirometry or absent reversibility does not rule out asthma
Serial PEFVariability >10% on twice-daily PEF over 2 weeksAlso useful for follow-up after starting treatment
Therapeutic trialSignificant reversibility after 4 weeks of ICSUseful when spirometry is equivocal
FeNO>50 ppb rules in asthma in the right clinical contextLower values cannot rule it out; suppressed by ICS and in smokers
BronchoprovocationRules out asthma with atypical presentation + normal spirometryNot routinely required

Not routine: chest X-ray (only if diagnosis in doubt, atypical symptoms, severe asthma, or comorbidity); peripheral eosinophilia and IgE (only in moderate–severe asthma); skin prick / RAST (helpful for identifying allergens to avoid, not for diagnosis).

FeNO interpretation: <25 ppb — eosinophilia unlikely · 25–49 ppb — possible · >50 ppb — likely. FeNO ≥20 ppb indicates type 2 inflammation and predicts biologic response. An unexpectedly high FeNO in a treated patient is a clue to poor ICS adherence.

Assessing control — the ACT

SINA recommends the Asthma Control Test at every clinical evaluation. It's a validated, self-administered, five-item questionnaire covering the past four weeks — and in SINA it does more than describe control, it determines where treatment begins.

Interactive

Ages 12 and older · each item scored 1 (worst) to 5 (best) · maximum 25 · recall period is the past 4 weeks

ACT scoreControl levelSINA implication
≥20ControlledTreatment-naïve → start at Step 1
16–19Partly controlledTreatment-naïve → start at Step 2
<16UncontrolledTreatment-naïve → start at Step 3

A change of ≥3 units is the minimal clinically important difference — use that, not any movement in the number, when judging whether a treatment change actually worked.

+

Assess future risk, not just current symptoms

  • Strongest predictor: severe attack in the past 12 months, or prior ICU admission — especially if intubated.
  • Modifiable risks: high reliever use, frequent OCS courses, low FEV₁, inadequate ICS, smoking/vaping, pregnancy, comorbidities, major psychological disorder, reduced socioeconomic status.
  • Risk of fixed airflow obstruction: inadequate ICS, tobacco/noxious exposure, low initial FEV₁, blood eosinophilia.
  • A well-controlled ACT score does not exclude high future risk — assess both.

Severity is assigned retrospectively, not at first visit — and only once control has been maintained on the minimum effective treatment for >3 months. Mild = controlled at Step 1–2 · Moderate = controlled at Step 3 · Severe = requires Step 4–5.

Where to start — treatment-naïve patients

This is SINA's signature contribution: rather than a vague severity impression, the ACT score sets the entry step. SABA alone is no longer recommended at any step.

1

ACT ≥20 → start at Step 1

  • Recommended: fixed-dose ICS/formoterol on an as-needed basis (Evidence A).
  • Alternative: SABA together with low-dose ICS as needed, from separate inhalers (Evidence B).
  • Escalate to daily low-dose ICS ± LABA if symptoms more than twice weekly, risk factors for exacerbation (severe attack in past 12 months, prior ICU admission), or evidence of fixed airway obstruction (Evidence B).
2

ACT 16–19 → start at Step 2

  • Fixed-dose combination low-dose ICS/LABA (Evidence A).
  • Plus reliever: SABA (± ICS) if maintenance is ICS/non-formoterol LABA — or ICS/formoterol from the same inhaler if a MART regimen is prescribed.
  • Confirm adherence explicitly before assuming the regimen has failed.
3

ACT <16 → start at Step 3

  • Fixed-dose combination medium-dose ICS/LABA as maintenance (Evidence A), with appropriate reliever.
  • If presenting with early signs of an exacerbation, consider a short course of oral prednisolone 30–60 mg for 3–5 days.
  • These patients need close follow-up to adjust treatment toward control.

Reassess at 1–3 month intervals after initiation. Relievers must be available to patients at every step — and rising reliever use is usually the earliest sign that control is slipping.

The five-step ladder

SINA is explicit that the steps are not compartments — they're a continuum built on patient engagement and monitoring. Step up when control isn't achieved; step down when it's been stable.

StepPreferredAlternatives & notes
Step 1 As-needed ICS/formoterol Alt: as-needed SABA + low-dose ICS (separate inhalers). SABA alone no longer recommended. Seasonal asthma: start low-dose ICS before the season.
Step 2 Daily low-dose ICS/LABA + as-needed reliever Alt: add LTRA to low-dose ICS (useful with concomitant rhinitis). Increasing ICS monotherapy to medium dose is generally less effective.
Step 3 Medium-dose ICS/LABA + reliever Alt: tiotropium (LAMA) combined with ICS where LABA can't be used — effect at least equivalent to LABA. Consult a specialist if control is difficult at this step.
Step 4 Single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT): ICS/LABA/LAMA SITT is cost-effective with better adherence than multiple-inhaler triple therapy. Alt: add tiotropium separately, or add LTRA (weaker evidence). High-dose ICS/LABA only if needed — step down when possible.
Step 5 Add biologic therapy Specialist referral strongly recommended. Early biologics may achieve remission and spare chronic OCS.
M

MART — using one inhaler for maintenance and relief

  • Applies only to ICS/formoterol combinations — formoterol's rapid onset is what makes it safe as a reliever.
  • Step 2: one inhalation twice daily as maintenance. Step 3: two inhalations twice daily.
  • Extra doses from the same device as reliever, up to 12 inhalations per day.
  • A patient needing that many doses for 2–3 days should seek medical advice to step up maintenance (Evidence A).

Critical safety point: ICS/formoterol must not be used as the reliever alongside an ICS/non-formoterol-LABA maintenance combination — GINA notes there's no evidence for efficacy or safety of that pairing.

B

Biologics at Step 5

  • Anti-IgE: omalizumab  ·  Anti-IL-5: mepolizumab  ·  Anti-IL-5R: benralizumab
  • Anti-IL-4Rα: dupilumab  ·  Anti-TSLP: tezepelumab (noted by SINA as not yet locally available — verify current Saudi availability)
  • Work-up before selection: clinical evaluation, ACT, peripheral eosinophil count, FeNO, IgE level, RAST.
  • Reassess response at 4–6 months against baseline ACT, exacerbation rate, hospitalizations, FEV₁, and daily OCS dose. No response → revisit the diagnosis, exclude coexisting conditions, confirm adherence, then consider switching agent.

GINA's two tracks — how they map onto SINA

GINA organizes adult treatment by which reliever is used, because the reliever choice should stay consistent as you step up and down. SINA's ladder is compatible; this is a useful lens rather than a competing system.

Track 1 — preferredTrack 2 — alternative
RelieverAs-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol (an anti-inflammatory reliever, "AIR")As-needed ICS-SABA, or as-needed SABA
Why preferredReduces severe exacerbation risk vs SABA-reliever regimens, with similar symptom control. Both the ICS and the formoterol contribute.Used when ICS-formoterol is unavailable or unsuitable
Practical advantageOne medication, one dose, one device for both reliever and maintenance across stepsRequires carrying and correctly using two separate inhalers

Regular SABA use is actively harmful, not merely inferior. GINA notes that even 1–2 weeks of regular SABA 2–4 times daily increases airway hyperresponsiveness and inflammation, heightens allergic response, and reduces bronchodilator effect.

When control isn't achieved

Before stepping up, work through this list — most "treatment failure" in primary care is something on it. Stepping up over an adherence or technique problem just adds cost and side effects.

  • Are the medication and dose appropriate for the step you intend?
  • Adherence — ask directly and non-judgmentally. An unexpectedly high FeNO is an objective clue to poor ICS adherence.
  • Inhaler technique — watch the patient actually use the device, every visit.
  • Device and spacer choice — is a spacer being used with the pMDI, and is it the right one?
  • Obstacles to taking treatment — cost, time, or the patient's own belief that they don't need it.
  • Environmental exposure at home — allergens, tobacco smoke, bukhoor, shisha.
  • Comorbidities — rhinosinusitis, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, GERD, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, anxiety, exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction.
  • Future risk of attacks and fixed airflow obstruction — reassess formally.
Reconsider the diagnosis itself
  • If a patient is genuinely adherent, using good technique, and still uncontrolled — ask whether this is asthma at all before escalating further.
  • Localized wheeze, crackles, stridor, clubbing, or murmurs all point elsewhere.

What changed in GINA 2026

Several 2026 revisions affect acute management directly — and two of them tighten practice that many of us learned the other way around. Worth reading even if you work primarily from SINA.

1

Oxygen thresholds revised downward

  • Supplemental oxygen is not recommended unless saturation is below 92%.
  • If oxygen is given to adults, adolescents, or children 6–11: upper limit of the target is 95%.
  • For children 5 and under with acute wheeze or severe exacerbation, target ≥92% if oxygen is given.

Note this differs from the SINA 2024 exacerbation guidance in our urgent care protocol, which states oxygen to keep SaO₂ ≥92% for severe presentations. The direction of travel in GINA is toward less liberal oxygen. Worth deciding locally which threshold your service follows and documenting it.

2

SABA doses are now more conservative

  • Driven by evidence of over-treatment during exacerbations.
  • SABA toxicity includes lactic acidosis, which causes compensatory hyperventilation — easily misread as worsening asthma, prompting yet more SABA.
  • Review the patient after the initial bronchodilator dose. If they've improved, they may not need more. Patients are too often written up for multiple doses without reassessment.
3

Anaphylaxis takes precedence

  • If a patient has features of anaphylaxis as well as asthma: give epinephrine first, then bronchodilators.
  • Epinephrine may be given intramuscularly or intranasally — the intranasal route is not suitable for small children.

4

Technique and device details that actually change dose delivered

  • Shake the salbutamol inhaler immediately before every actuation. Failing to do so can deliver inadvertently ultra-high doses from a new inhaler, or low doses from a well-used one. Any suspension-formulated inhaler must be shaken before each actuation.
  • Tidal breathing technique with pMDI + spacer is the usual route in acute asthma and is frequently performed incorrectly — teach and observe it.
5

Every exacerbation is a red flag

  • Even a single exacerbation requiring urgent care or OCS should trigger a full review of maintenance treatment.
  • ICS-formoterol is now an option for mild exacerbations in primary care/ED — an opportunity to start an anti-inflammatory reliever (preferably MART) before the patient leaves.
  • Measure lung function (spirometry, or PEF if unavailable) for all patients except preschool children and life-threatening presentations — and again before discharge.
  • An acute presentation is also a chance to confirm and document the diagnosis with pre/post-bronchodilator lung function.
  • OCS stewardship: optimize inhaled therapy, use biologics where indicated, use appropriate dose and duration, avoid maintenance OCS except as a last resort, and monitor cumulative lifetime exposure.

Referral & acute crossover

  • Difficulty achieving control at Step 3 — consult an asthma specialist (SINA, Evidence D).
  • Requiring Step 5 / biologic consideration — specialist referral strongly recommended.
  • Diagnostic uncertainty after adequate trial, or features suggesting an alternative diagnosis.
  • Occupational asthma suspected — needs specialist assessment and workplace evaluation.
  • Any severe exacerbation, ICU admission, or intubation history — flag as high future risk and manage proactively.

Using the action plan well

  • SINA lists a written action plan as a core component of asthma management — not an optional extra.
  • Fill it in with the patient rather than handing over a blank form: the actual inhaler names they hold, the actual doses, the actual prednisolone course you'd want them to start.
  • Complete the emergency phone numbers at the bottom before they leave the room.
  • Read zone D (danger) aloud together — severe breathlessness, inability to complete a sentence, blue lips or fingertips. That's the part that has to be recognized under stress, at home, at 3am.
