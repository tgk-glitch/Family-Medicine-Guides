GINA 2026 · Two Age Bands, One Console

Asthma in the very young and school-age children

A working guide to the two pediatric age bands GINA treats differently at almost every turn — assessment, diagnosis, differential, the preferred-vs-alternative treatment tracks, acute management, and follow-up. Flip the switch below to view one band, or hold both side-by-side to see exactly where they diverge.

≤5 years · clinical diagnosis, no spirometry 6–11 years · spirometry + two-track therapy 📊 PRAM severity built in
Viewing
Both bands shown — watch for the DIVERGE flags.
01 — ORIENTATION

Why split children into two bands at all?

The dividing line is not arbitrary. Around age 5–6, two things change that reshape the entire approach: children can finally perform reproducible spirometry, and the evidence base for ICS-formoterol regimens catches up. Below that line, asthma is a clinical diagnosis and treatment is deliberately conservative.

🔗

Analogy · Two different courtrooms

Diagnosing the 6–11 year-old is like a trial with forensic evidence — you can put lung function on the stand and get an objective reading. Diagnosing the ≤5 year-old is a trial on eyewitness testimony alone: history, observed wheeze, and response to a treatment trial. Same crime (variable airflow obstruction), but one court has instruments and the other runs on careful observation — so the burden of proof is structured differently.

⚠️

Trap · Don't anchor on "viral-induced" vs "multiple-trigger" wheeze

Older teaching sorted preschool wheeze into tidy phenotypes (episodic viral wheeze vs multiple-trigger wheeze; transient vs persistent vs late-onset). GINA 2026 explicitly downgrades these: they have poor predictive value, overlap with non-asthma conditions, and some can only be applied retrospectively. Use them to describe, never to decide. The clinical job is to diagnose this individual child using the criteria — not to file them into a phenotype box.

Exam QA 9-month-old has their first-ever episode of wheeze during a URTI, with crackles on auscultation. What is the single most likely diagnosis, and does this meet criteria for asthma?
Bronchiolitis — not asthma. A first wheeze under 12 months, especially with crackles, is bronchiolitis until proven otherwise. It fails asthma criteria on two counts: it is a single (not recurrent) episode, and crackles point to an alternative cause. GINA's rule of thumb: the younger the child, the higher the probability of an alternative diagnosis.
02 — ASSESSMENT

Taking the history: what you ask, and who you ask

Assessment means building the picture of variable respiratory symptoms + triggers + response. In the younger band, every data point is filtered through a parent/caregiver — which is both your only channel and your biggest source of error.

5⁻≤5 yearsHistory via parent/caregiver
  • How an episode starts & evolves: wheeze (high-pitched, on breathing out), dry cough, coughing spells, difficult/heavy/fast breathing.
  • Timing that points to asthma: worse in sleep, after laughing, crying, or activity. In infants, crying and laughing = the exertion equivalent of exercise.
  • Interval symptoms: between colds, any cough/wheeze after running or during sleep? Does the child stop playing early?
  • Triggers: colds, cold air, animals/allergens, activity.
  • Episode duration: minutes, or up to 24 hours? (Acute wheezing episodes typically last >24 h.)
  • Treatment response already seen: did a reliever help, and how fast (20–60 min is typical of asthma)?
  • Type-2 clues: eczema, food allergy, hay fever; first-degree relative with atopy/asthma.
Parents/caregivers may report irritability, tiredness, or mood change as the main problem — not "wheeze." Some cultures/languages have no word for wheeze at all.
116–11 yearsChild can self-report + objective testing
  • Typical variable symptoms: wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough — variable over time and in intensity.
  • Pattern: often worse at night/on waking; triggered by exercise, laughter, allergens, cold air.
  • The very distinctive clue: symptoms worsen after stopping exercise.
  • Often appear/worsen with viral infections.
  • Quantify control: daytime symptoms/week, night waking, reliever use/week, activity limitation (feeds the control grid).
  • Objective anchor available: plan spirometry with reversibility (see Diagnose).
  • Type-2 clues + biomarkers: atopy history; FeNO >35 ppb or blood eosinophils support Type-2 asthma.
Use a validated instrument: Childhood Asthma Control Test (C-ACT) is validated from age 4–11.
⚡

High-Yield · The single most specific sign in the young child

Audible wheeze — heard without a stethoscope — that recurs, and occurs during sleep or with activity/laughing/crying — is the most specific asthma sign in ≤5s. But it must be confirmed at least once: directly by a clinician, via a video/audio recording, or by a caregiver who has proven they can tell wheeze from other noisy breathing.

⚠️

Trap · "Wheeze" is a loaded word

Caregivers use "wheeze" for any noisy breathing — nasal congestion, throat clearing, stridor (upper airway), or bronchial secretions (lower airway) — none of which is true bronchoconstriction. Never take reported "wheeze" at face value in a young child. Confirm it, or your diagnosis is built on sand.

03 — EXAMINATION

What the physical exam does — and doesn't — give you

Between episodes, the chest is usually normal in both bands. The exam earns its keep during an acute episode (confirming lower-airway obstruction) and in hunting for red-flag features that argue against asthma.

Signs of lower-airway obstruction (supports asthma)

  • • Expiratory wheeze / rhonchi
  • • Accessory muscle use, prolonged expiration
  • • Decreased air entry / air exchange
  • • In severe cases: low SpO₂, cyanosis, rising CO₂
Timely response to bronchodilator seals it

Between-episode exam: what "normal" means

  • • Chest frequently normal — this does not rule out asthma
  • • Look for allergic stigmata: eczema, allergic rhinitis
  • • Check growth (height) — a treatment-safety baseline
Measure & plot height yearly in ≤5s on ICS
🔎

Going Further · Red-flag exam findings that mean "not just asthma" (≤5y referral list, Box 10-5)

Any of these in a young child points to an alternative diagnosis and warrants investigation/referral: failure to thrive · neonatal or very-early onset (especially with failure to thrive) · vomiting with respiratory symptoms · continuous wheeze, recurrent stridor, or a seal-like barking cough (airway malacia) · no response to asthma meds · symptoms with no link to typical triggers like viral URTI · focal lung or cardiovascular signs, or finger clubbing · hypoxemia (<95%) when awake, outside an exacerbation.

⚡

High-Yield · A quiet chest is the loudest sign

During an acute episode, a silent chest is not reassurance — it means airflow is too low to generate a wheeze. Paired with agitation, drowsiness, or confusion (signs of cerebral hypoxemia), it is a life-threatening emergency in either band.

04 — DIAGNOSIS

Confirming the diagnosis — two genuinely different rulebooks ⇄ Diverge

This is the sharpest divergence between the bands. The younger child is diagnosed on a 3-criteria clinical rule; the school-age child gets the adult-style objective rule built on documented variable airflow.

5⁻≤5 years · ALL 3 criteria requiredBox 10-2 — else record "suspected asthma"
  • ① Recurrent acute wheezing episodes. ≥2 acute episodes in 12 months, OR ≥1 acute episode plus interval symptoms. Wheeze must be confirmed at least once.
  • ② No likely alternative cause (a concurrent viral URTI is allowed). Imaging/labs usually not required.
  • ③ Timely response to asthma treatment. Improvement within 20–60 min of SABA (or 3–4 h after SABA+OCS in a severe episode), OR a clear decrease in symptoms during a 2–3 month ICS trial.
Suspected asthma: if only 1–2 criteria are met, label it "suspected," treat, and reassess over time. A definitive label often isn't possible on day one.
116–11 years · symptoms + variable airflowBox 1-2 — objective confirmation
  • ① History of typical variable symptoms (wheeze, SOB, chest tightness, cough) that vary over time and intensity.
  • ② Confirmed variable expiratory airflow limitation, by any one of:
  • Bronchodilator reversibility: FEV₁ rises ≥12% predicted (or PEF ≥15%) after salbutamol.
  • Diurnal PEF variability >13% (children) averaged over 2 weeks.
  • Rise after 4 weeks ICS: FEV₁ ≥12% predicted (or PEF ≥15%).
  • Bronchial challenge: FEV₁ fall ≥20% (methacholine) or ≥15% (exercise/mannitol/saline).
Biomarkers to support (not confirm): FeNO >35 ppb or blood eosinophils above reference support Type-2 asthma — but low levels never rule asthma out.
⚠️

Trap · Normal spirometry does NOT exclude asthma (6–11y)

Lung function is variable by definition — it can be completely normal between episodes. If suspicion is high and the first test is normal, repeat during symptoms, withhold bronchodilator first (SABA ≥4 h; long-acting 24–48 h), or move to challenge testing. One normal FEV₁ is not an acquittal.

⚡

High-Yield · The reversibility number differs by age

Children (6–11y): FEV₁ ≥12% predicted defines a positive bronchodilator response — note it's "% predicted," and there is no absolute-mL requirement as in adults (where it's ≥12% and ≥200 mL). This is a classic exam discriminator.

Exam QA 3-year-old has had two clinician-confirmed wheezing episodes this year and coughs at night between colds, but you cannot yet document a treatment response. What do you record, and what's the next step?
Record "suspected asthma." Criteria ① (recurrent episodes + interval symptoms) and ② (no alternative cause) are met, but ③ (documented treatment response) is not yet fulfilled. Next step: a 2–3 month trial of daily low-dose ICS + as-needed SABA, ideally timed to the child's symptomatic season, then reassess. A convincing response confirms asthma; then reduce ICS to the minimum effective dose.

High-Yield · Three pattern-recognition shortcuts

Sudden + unilateral wheeze → foreign body. Wet/productive cough as the only symptom → not asthma (think PBB, CF, suppurative disease). First wheeze <12 months → bronchiolitis. These three patterns defuse the most common "asthma" misdiagnoses on exams and in clinic.

🔗

Analogy · Asthma is a diagnosis of pattern, not a single snapshot

A mimic is a photograph — one fixed finding (a murmur, clubbing, a unilateral wheeze, failure to thrive). Asthma is a flip-book — the same reversible obstruction recurring and varying over time. When a "photograph" finding dominates and won't animate into the asthma flip-book, stop and investigate the mimic.

06 — CONTROL

Assessing control: same 4 questions, different reliever threshold

Both bands assess control over the last 4 weeks across the same four domains, then layer on future-risk factors. Count how many of the four are "yes."

Symptom control — ≤5 years (Box 11-1)

In the past 4 weeks, has the child had:

  • • Daytime symptoms more than twice a week?
  • • Any night waking or night coughing due to asthma?
  • • Reliever needed more than twice a week?
  • • Any activity limitation? (runs/plays less, tires on walks)
None = Well 1–2 = Partly 3–4 = Uncontrolled

Symptom control — 6–11 years (Box 2-2)

In the past 4 weeks, has the child had:

  • • Daytime symptoms more than twice a week?
  • • Any night waking due to asthma?
  • • Reliever needed more than twice a week?
  • • Any activity limitation due to asthma?
None = Well 1–2 = Partly 3–4 = Uncontrolled
⚠️

Trap · The "SABA more than twice a week" line excludes pre-exercise dosing

When you count reliever use for control, don't count a puff taken before sport — that's prophylaxis, not rescue. And before ever stepping treatment up, confirm the symptoms are truly asthma, and that inhaler technique and adherence are adequate — the two commonest reasons for apparent "uncontrolled" asthma.

🔎

Going Further · Future-risk factors (assessed even when symptoms look controlled)

Exacerbation risk: ≥1 severe episode (ED/hospital/OCS) in the past year · uncontrolled symptoms · start of the child's "flare season" (especially autumn) · indoor tobacco smoke, allergens, pollution — especially combined with viral infection · poor adherence/technique · major psychosocial or socioeconomic problems. Persistent airflow-limitation risk: severe asthma with several hospitalizations; history of bronchiolitis. Even months of good control doesn't zero the risk — viral URTIs still trigger flares.

🔗

Analogy · Control has two dials, not one

Think of a mixing desk with two sliders: "symptoms now" and "risk later." A child can look great on the first slider (quiet last month) while the second is pushed high (an ED visit in spring, a smoker at home, autumn approaching). Manage both sliders — treating only today's symptoms leaves the child exposed to the next viral-triggered flare.

07 — TREATMENT

Stepwise control: preferred vs alternative, by band ⇄ Diverge

Here the bands separate most in ceiling and tooling. The 6–11 band gets the modern ICS-formoterol (AIR / MART) machinery and runs to Step 5. The ≤5 band has a deliberately short, conservative ladder — no MART evidence exists below age 5 — that tops out at "double the low ICS dose, then refer."

⚡

High-Yield · The one-line summary of the whole ladder

6–11: anti-inflammatory reliever (ICS-formoterol) is now the preferred backbone, and MART runs Steps 3–5. ≤5: plain daily low-dose ICS is the preferred controller, there is no MART, and the ladder is short — Step 3 is literally "double the low dose," Step 4 is "refer." Whenever control fails in either band, the first move is not escalation — it's checking technique, adherence, diagnosis, and triggers.

⚠️

Trap · "Low dose" is not interchangeable between drugs

These bands look tidy, but they do not imply equal potency. Swapping a child from "low-dose" drug A to "low-dose" drug B may quietly raise or lower their effective steroid exposure — destabilizing control or adding side-effect risk. Change one variable at a time and reassess.

Exam QAn 8-year-old on daily low-dose ICS still wakes twice weekly with cough and uses his reliever most days. Technique and adherence are confirmed good. What is the single preferred next step?
Step up to low-dose ICS-formoterol MART (Step 3 preferred) — the same inhaler for daily maintenance and as-needed relief. Alternatives are low-dose ICS-LABA or medium-dose ICS with a PRN reliever. The stem deliberately pre-confirms technique and adherence so that escalation is the correct answer rather than the usual "recheck the basics first."
🔎

Going Further · The montelukast (LTRA) counseling point

LTRA appears as an alternative in both bands but is consistently less effective than ICS for preventing exacerbations. Whenever you prescribe it, GINA now asks you to actively counsel families about neuropsychiatric adverse effects (sleep disturbance, mood/behavior change) — a boxed-warning-level talking point that shows up in exams.

08 — ACUTE

The exacerbation: grade it, treat it, know when to transfer

Assess severity while you start treatment — never sequentially. GINA recommends a validated score; PRAM (Pediatric Respiratory Assessment Measure, validated ages 2–17) is the flagship. The severity thresholds differ by band — most importantly the respiratory-rate cut-off.

⚡

High-Yield · The age-specific vital-sign cut-offs

In ≤5 years, the severe/transfer respiratory-rate flag is >40/min (normal is <40 in 1–5y; <50 in infants 2–12 months). In the older/adult framework it's >30/min. The SpO₂ <92% on room air threshold is shared and is a hospital-transfer trigger in the young child. Remember pulse oximetry can overestimate saturation in children with dark skin.

⚠️

Trap · Two "who does what" errors in the young child

(1) GINA does not recommend caregiver-initiated oral corticosteroids at home for ≤5s — the evidence is weak and cumulative-OCS harm is real. (2) Seek same-day medical care if the child needs SABA <4-hourly or on >3 occasions in 12 hours, and urgent care if a single set of 4 puffs doesn't hold. These "seek care" triggers are frequently tested.

🔎

Going Further · Immediate hospital-transfer criteria (≤5y, Box 12-3)

Transfer now if any of: cyanosis · unable to speak or drink · RR >40/min · SpO₂ <92% on room air · quiet chest when dyspneic — or failure to respond to 4–6 salbutamol puffs given as separate puffs every 20 min ×3 over 1 hour, or persisting tachypnea despite three SABA sets. Add a low threshold for children <2 years (dehydration + respiratory fatigue risk) and those with prior life-threatening episodes. Keep giving SABA, O₂, and start systemic steroids during transfer.

Exam QA 4-year-old in the ED has SpO₂ 90%, RR 46, scalene retractions, and inspiratory + expiratory wheeze. Beyond oxygen and repeated salbutamol, name the two additional first-line drugs and one dosing caveat.
This is a severe exacerbation (SpO₂ <92%, RR >40). Add ipratropium bromide (4 puffs / 0.25 mg neb with each SABA set, up to 3×) and a systemic corticosteroid — prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg. Caveat: the OCS ceiling is age-banded — max 30 mg for a 2–5-year-old (20 mg if <2y). Consider IV magnesium sulfate (≥2y) if severe/refractory, and arrange hospital transfer.
09 — FOLLOW-UP

After treatment: the assess–adjust–review cycle

Management is a loop, not a prescription. Two timelines matter: the post-exacerbation follow-up (catch the relapse) and the routine review cycle (right-size the controller as the child changes — because preschool symptoms often remit).

Post-exacerbation (≤5y, Box 12)
Next 24–48 hours
Caregiver seeks care if no improvement or any deterioration. Written action plan in hand.
Within 1–3 days
Arrange review after the flare — confirm recovery, reinforce technique, document the diagnosis if now met.
2–3 months later
Second review — reassess control and future risk; the child has had one flare and is at risk of another.
Routine review cycle (both bands)
2–3 months after any start/change
Review response to a new controller or dose before deciding to continue or adjust.
Every 3–6 months
Reassess whether controller is still needed (≤5 symptoms often remit); measure & plot height yearly on ICS.
Step-down → 3–6 weeks later

Seasonal

⚡

High-Yield · Before every step-up, run the four-item checklist

Diagnosis · Technique · Adherence · Triggers. GINA repeats this at every escalation point in both bands. Most "treatment failure" is one of these four — not an inadequate drug. Step up the medication only after these are confirmed.

🔗

Analogy · Controller dosing is a thermostat, not a light switch

You don't leave the heating on max all winter and forget it. You set it, watch the room, and dial it down once it's comfortable — then nudge it back up in a cold snap. ICS in a young child is the same: reach control, step down to the minimum effective dose, and be ready to step back up for the flare season. "Set and forget" over-treats the child who has quietly remitted.

🔎

Going Further · "Will they grow out of it?" — how to answer honestly

Preschool symptoms remit in a substantial proportion of children, but remission is not cure — asthma often recurs later, and previously-affected lungs carry a higher risk of accelerated decline (synergistic with smoking). GINA's advised phrasing: "asthma has gone quiet for now." If you say "remission," explain it, because families often hear it as "cured forever." Keep the conversation on the child's current benefit from treatment — fewer attacks, school attendance, normal play — not promises about the future.

The quiet chest is the ominous sign

A wheeze fading toward a silent chest is not improvement — airflow is too low to generate sound. Combined with agitation heading toward drowsiness, it signals a slide from severe toward life-threatening. Escalate; do not reassure.

Saudi practice · SINA 2024

Childhood ACT & the SINA pediatric ladder

GINA sets the age-band framework above. SINA adds the tool Saudi practice actually runs on — the C-ACT for ages 5–12, and TRACK for children under 5 — and uses the score to decide the starting step, exactly as it does in adults.

Interactive

Childhood Asthma Control Test (C-ACT)

Ages 5–12 · seven items · child answers the first four, caregiver the last three · total 0–27

Part 1 — answered by the child (0–3 each)
Part 2 — answered by the caregiver (0–5 each, past 4 weeks)

SINA starting step for children ≥5

Control status Start at Recommended option
Controlled · C-ACT ≥20 Step 1 As-needed ICS whenever SABA is needed. For intermittent viral-induced wheeze, as-needed SABA alone is acceptable.
Partly controlled / uncontrolled · C-ACT <20 Step 2 Regular low-dose ICS if symptoms more than twice weekly (Evidence A). If the child cannot or will not use ICS, LTRA is an option — but less effective (Evidence B).
Uncontrolled with more severe symptoms Step 3 Low-dose ICS/LABA — either MART with ICS/formoterol, or regular ICS/non-formoterol LABA with SABA as reliever.

Score interpretation: C-ACT ≤19 indicates asthma that is not adequately controlled. SINA recommends the questionnaire be completed before the physician evaluation, and treats it as complementary to — never a replacement for — clinical assessment.

Under 5 years: SINA recommends TRACK (Respiratory and Asthma Control in Kids) rather than C-ACT. Note this aligns with the GINA guidance above: no MART under age 5.
