The skeleton every station shares

Introduce & rapport→ History→ Red flags→ Physical exam→ Investigations→ Management→ Plan & closing

This structure repeats across all 107 stations in the source manual, which is itself the highest-yield thing to internalize: examiners are marking whether you move through it, not just whether you know the disease. Two elements appear in almost every history: ICEE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations, Effect) and, at closing, explicit red-flag/safety-net counseling plus a concrete follow-up plan. Skipping either costs marks even with a correct diagnosis.

Hypertension

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport — name, age, marital status, occupation.

•History

  • Chief complaint: open-ended, then clarify. Most patients are asymptomatic — this is often a routine or follow-up visit, not a presenting complaint.
  • HPI — SOCRATES if symptomatic. If this is a follow-up, assess the 3 C's: Control (recent home readings), Compliance (medications, doses, side effects — dizziness, cough, swelling — plus lifestyle), Complications (end-organ damage).
  • If hypertensive emergency: stabilize and refer to ER — don't continue the routine history.
🚩 Red flags
  • End-organ damage — ask by system: cerebrovascular (headache, dizziness, visual change, confusion, focal weakness, seizures); cardiovascular (chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, orthopnea, PND); renal (swelling, oliguria, hematuria); retinopathy (visual disturbance/loss); peripheral/aortic (claudication, cold extremities, syncope).
  • Screen for secondary causes if: acute rise from previously stable readings, onset before puberty or age <30 without risk factors, malignant/accelerated HTN with end-organ signs, severe (>180/120), or resistant to ≥3 drugs.
  • OSA (snoring, apneic episodes, daytime somnolence) · coarctation (headache, epistaxis, claudication, cold legs) · renal disease (hematuria, frothy urine, flank pain) · hyperaldosteronism (weakness, spasms) · pheochromocytoma (headache, palpitations, sweating) · Cushing's (central weight gain, hirsutism, bruising) · thyroid disease.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: BP, HR, RR, temp, height, weight, BMI, waist circumference.
  • StageCriteria
    ElevatedSBP 120–129 and DBP <80
    Stage 1SBP 130–139 or DBP 80–89
    Stage 2SBP ≥140 or DBP ≥90
    CrisisSBP >180 or DBP >120 — urgency (no end-organ damage) vs emergency (with it)
  • Gold standard for diagnosis: 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM). In-clinic readings are for screening only.
  • Focused exam for end-organ damage: neuro (cranial nerves, motor/sensory, reflexes, MMSE), CVS (murmurs, gallops, JVD, peripheral edema, crackles, diminished pulses, carotid/femoral bruits, inter-arm BP difference), renal (edema/volume overload), fundoscopy (hemorrhages, cotton wool spots, papilledema).
  • Signs of secondary HTN: coarctation — infraclavicular systolic murmur, radiofemoral delay, cold legs. PCKD — palpable kidneys. Renal artery stenosis — renal bruit. Pheochromocytoma — café-au-lait spots, flushing. Cushing's — buffalo hump, moon face, striae. Hyperthyroid — tremor, palmar erythema, goiter.

🔬Investigations

  • Initial: CBC · U&E, Ca, urea, creatinine + eGFR · fasting lipids & glucose · TSH/fT4 · urinalysis + urine albumin:creatinine ratio · CXR · ECG.
  • Additional: renal/aortic ultrasound, echo, CT/MRI brain, ABI — as end-organ or secondary-cause suspicion directs.
  • 10-year ASCVD risk calculator (MDCalc) drives the treatment threshold.

💊Management

  • Non-pharm: DASH diet, sodium <1500 mg/day, weight loss 5–10%, exercise ≥30 min most days, smoking cessation.
  • Pharmacological threshold: Stage 1 + ASCVD risk ≥10%, or Stage 2 — treat. First-line: thiazide, CCB, ACEi/ARB.
  • SituationApproach
    Stage 1Single first-line agent, titrate/add as needed
    Stage 2Two first-line agents from different classes — ideally single-pill combination
    Resistant (>3 drugs incl. diuretic)Check adherence, exogenous causes (caffeine, NSAIDs, OCP, steroids, decongestants) first. Switch to chlorthalidone; add spironolactone (preferred)
    Diabetes/CKD, eGFR <60 or albuminuria >30 mg/gACEi or ARB
    Heart failureACEi/ARB + beta-blocker + MRA
    PregnancyLabetalol, methyldopa, nifedipine — avoid ACEi/ARB
  • Referral: emergency/cardiology for crisis · nephrology for resistant HTN · nephrology/endocrine/pulmonology for secondary causes · cardiology/nephrology/neuro/ophtho for end-organ damage · obstetrics for pre-eclampsia/eclampsia.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Confirm understanding — self-management support, home BP monitoring.
  • Explain red flags and safety-net.
  • Medication counseling: timing, dosing, managing side effects.
  • Periodic health maintenance appropriate to age.
  • Follow-up: every 2–4 weeks until BP at goal, then every 3–6 months; monitor electrolytes/renal function on ACEi/ARB/diuretics.
Endocrine Source pp. 14–21

Diabetes Mellitus

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • HPI — SOCRATES, plus ask specifically whether this presentation is hyperglycemic, hypoglycemic, DKA, or HHS.
  • Hyperglycemic: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss.
  • Hypoglycemic: sweating, palpitations, pallor, tremor, ↓consciousness, syncope, headache, hunger — ask frequency and trigger.
  • DKA: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, Kussmaul breathing, dizziness, ↓consciousness.
  • HHS: typically an older adult, altered mental status, hypovolemia.
  • If DKA/HHS: stabilize and refer to ER — don't continue routine history.
  • Follow-up visit: onset/diagnosis circumstances, current follow-up provider, last HbA1c, then the 3 C's — Control, Compliance (meds + lifestyle), Complications.
🚩 Red flags
  • ~20% of T2DM patients already have microvascular complications at diagnosis — screen even if asymptomatic.
  • Microvascular: nephropathy (usually lab-diagnosed, ↓urine output); retinopathy (blurred/lost vision); neuropathy (symmetric numbness/burning feet, ulcers, falls); autonomic (hypo/angina unawareness, gastroparesis, achalasia, constipation, ED).
  • Macrovascular: CAD (chest pain, HF symptoms), CVA (focal weakness), PAD (claudication, hair loss, discoloration, ulcer).
  • Recurrent candidal infections, skin infections, UTIs — think undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: BP, HR, RR, temp, height, weight, BMI, waist circumference.
  • Skin: acanthosis nigricans, skin tags. Eyes: visual acuity. Mouth: hygiene, ulcers, candidiasis, leukoplakia. Neck: thyroid, nodes. Shoulder: frozen shoulder (restricted ROM). CVS: distal pulses, carotid bruits, CABG scar, HF signs.
  • Comprehensive foot exam — every visit: inspection (skin integrity, callus, deformity, ulcer, nails); PAD screen (dorsalis pedis + posterior tibial pulses → ABI if diminished); temperature, vibration, pinprick, and 10-g monofilament testing.

🔬Investigations

  • Diagnostic criterionThreshold
    HbA1c≥6.5%
    Fasting plasma glucose≥126 mg/dL
    Random plasma glucose≥200 mg/dL (with classic symptoms/crisis)
    2-h OGTT≥200 mg/dL
  • Fasting lipids (ASCVD risk + statin monitoring) · U&E/eGFR · LFTs (NAFLD, statin safety) · urine albumin:creatinine ratio · CBC · ECG · ABI (≤0.9 abnormal) · DEXA every 2–3y if >65.

💊Management

  • Non-pharm: diet (address barriers; limit protein 0.8 g/kg if CKD); exercise ≥150 min/week moderate-vigorous over ≥3 days, no >2 consecutive rest days, plus 2–3 resistance sessions/week. Caution: avoid vigorous exercise with retinopathy (vitreous hemorrhage/detachment risk); proper footwear with neuropathy; screen for CVD before vigorous activity with autonomic neuropathy.
  • Glycemic targets: A1c near target (<7.5%) → 3–6 month lifestyle trial first. A1c ≥7.5–8% → start pharmacotherapy at diagnosis.
  • First-line: metformin 500 mg OD with breakfast, titrate to 2 g/day divided — unless ASCVD, HF, or CKD is present, in which case start a GLP-1 RA and/or SGLT2i independent of baseline A1c or metformin use.
  • Insulin if presenting glucose ≥300, A1c >10%, or hyperglycemic symptoms with catabolism.
  • Agent classPriority use
    GLP-1 RA (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide)ASCVD or high CV risk; strong weight-loss benefit
    SGLT2i (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin)HFrEF/HFpEF and/or CKD (eGFR ≥20) — slows CKD progression, reduces HF admission independent of A1c
    Both togetherIf ASCVD + CKD/HF both present
  • Avoid for weight loss goals: sulfonylureas, insulin, TZDs (cause weight gain).
  • Complication prevention: BP <130/80 (ACEi/ARB if proteinuria/CKD); statin if >40y or younger with high risk; aspirin 75–162 mg only if established ASCVD; dilated eye exam annually; comprehensive foot exam annually + daily self-care; annual UACR + eGFR; influenza, pneumococcal, hepatitis B, COVID, zoster (≥50y) vaccination.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Self-management support; home glucose monitoring if starting insulin; CGM encouraged for insulin-treated patients.
  • Medication timing, dosing, side-effect management; foot care education.
  • Explain hyper/hypoglycemic symptom recognition and safety-net.
  • Follow-up: every 3 months if changing therapy or off-target; every 6 months if stable.
  • Refer: endocrinology (poor control, new T1DM/LADA, recurrent severe hypoglycemia); nephro/neuro/cardio/ophtho for complications; podiatry/vascular/ID for foot disease; obstetrics if pregnant; surgery if BMI ≥40 (or ≥35 with poor control) for bariatric evaluation.
Cardiovascular Source pp. 98–102

Chest Pain

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • SOCRATES — the differential lives almost entirely in this mnemonic for chest pain:
  • FeaturePoints toward
    Sudden onsetMI, PE, aortic dissection
    Gradual onsetStable angina, GERD
    Crushing/pressureIschemic
    Sharp/pleuriticPE, pericarditis
    TearingAortic dissection
    BurningGERD
    Radiates to arm/jaw/neckAngina/MI
    Radiates to backAortic dissection
    Exertional, <20 min, relieved by rest/GTNStable angina
    Continuous >20 minMI
    Worse lying down, better leaning forwardPericarditis
    Worse with inspiration/coughPE, pericarditis
🚩 Red flags
  • Hypotension or syncope with the pain · diaphoresis + nausea + radiation (ACS pattern) · tearing pain radiating to back (dissection) · sudden pleuritic pain + dyspnea (PE/pneumothorax) · new ST changes or arrhythmia on ECG.
  • Don't miss the atypical presentation — women, elderly, and diabetics may present with dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, fatigue, or pain in the shoulder/neck/jaw/back instead of classic chest pain.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: appearance (anxiety, pallor, sweating, tremor), BP in both arms (dissection), HR character/regularity, RR, temp.
  • Cardiac: scars/pacemaker, JVP, apex beat displacement, heaves/thrills, murmurs, pericardial rub, S3/S4.
  • Respiratory: accessory muscle use, tracheal position, decreased breath sounds (pneumothorax), basal crackles (LV failure/pneumonia), wheeze, percussion for effusion.
  • Abdominal: hepatomegaly (congestive HF), epigastric tenderness (PUD/GERD). MSK: reproducible chest wall tenderness.

🔬Investigations

  • Urgent (all suspected ACS): 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes · troponin I/T · CXR (pneumothorax, pneumonia, widened mediastinum) · CBC, electrolytes, renal function · SpO₂/ABG if hypoxic.
  • Additional: echo (effusion, wall motion), CT angiography (PE, dissection), stress test/angiography (stable angina), endoscopy (suspected GERD/PUD).

💊Management

  • ⚠ ACS/angina — MONA-BASH

    Morphine (2–4 mg IV, titrate) · Oxygen (if hypoxic only) · Nitroglycerin 0.4 mg q5min ×3 (avoid in severe AS, HCM, suspected RV infarct, hypotension, marked brady/tachycardia, or PDE5-inhibitor use within 24h) · Antiplatelets (aspirin 162–325 mg + clopidogrel 600 mg or ticagrelor) — Beta-blockers, ACEi, Statins, Heparin.

    PCI is the treatment of choice if door-to-needle <90 min; thrombolysis for STEMI/new LBBB within 12h if PCI unavailable within 90 min.

  • DiagnosisTreatment
    PericarditisNSAIDs (ibuprofen 600 mg TID) + colchicine 0.5 mg BID
    Aortic dissectionIV beta-blocker (esmolol infusion) + urgent surgical referral
    PEAnticoagulation (LMWH or DOAC)
    PneumothoraxNeedle decompression / chest tube if large or symptomatic
    GERDPPI (omeprazole 20–40 mg daily)
    MSK painNSAIDs, local heat, reassurance
    Anxiety/panicBreathing techniques, CBT, SSRI if recurrent

✅Plan & Closing

  • ER admission: ACS, PE, dissection, pneumothorax, severe pneumonia.
  • Refer: cardiology (risk modification, stress testing, post-ACS), GI (refractory GERD), psychiatry (recurrent panic-related pain).
  • Explain red flags/safety-net; discuss periodic health maintenance; arrange post-MI follow-up care.
Neurology Source pp. 36–39

Headache

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • SOCRATES, with headache-specific detail:
  • FeaturePoints toward
    Unilateral throbbing ± auraMigraine
    Bilateral, tight/pressure bandTension
    Unilateral retro-orbital, red watery eye, ptosis, worse at nightCluster
    Sudden 'thunderclap' (peaks <1 min)Subarachnoid hemorrhage
    Worse early morning, progressiveRaised ICP
    Scalp tenderness on touch, age >50Temporal arteritis
    Radiates to neckSAH
  • Cluster pattern: daily for 6–12 weeks, severe unilateral retro-orbital pain, worse at night, red watery eye, ptosis, rhinorrhea/congestion.
  • Tension: episodic or chronic (>15 days/month), linked to emotional stress, generalized pressure ± neck radiation.
⚠ SNOOP4 — the red-flag mnemonic examiners expect
  • Systemic symptoms/illness — fever, weight loss, night sweats, immunocompromise
  • Neurological signs — focal deficits, altered mental status, seizures
  • Onset sudden — thunderclap → suspect SAH
  • Older age at onset — new headache >50y → temporal arteritis, mass lesion
  • Pattern change/Progression — first or worst headache; change in frequency/severity/features
  • Positional — worse lying/standing → intracranial hypertension or CSF leak
  • Precipitated by Valsalva/exertion — cough, sneeze, exercise → Chiari, intracranial lesion
  • Papilledema — raised ICP

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: BP, HR, RR, temp, height, weight, BMI, waist circumference.
  • Head & neck: temporal artery tenderness (arteritis), frontal/maxillary sinus tenderness, TMJ tenderness/crepitus.
  • Meningeal signs if suspected. Full neurological exam including fundoscopy and otoscopy. HENT.

Medication-overuse headacheChronic analgesic use (NSAID/paracetamol/codeine/ergotamine) or GTN/nifedipineNormal exam; taper offending drug
Temporal arteritis (GCA)Age >50, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, acute visual loss, ±polymyalgiaImmediate high-dose steroids + urgent ophthalmology
Malignant hypertensionBP >200/130, visual disturbance, encephalopathy, proteinuriaAdmit for BP control
Intracranial tumorNew, worse in AM, progressive, personality change, ±seizures, age >50Urgent neurology referral

🔬Investigations

  • Not indicated for primary headaches. CBC/CRP/ESR if temporal arteritis suspected. CT/MRI if red flags present. LP if infection, malignancy, or hemorrhage suspected.

💊Management

  • TypeAcute treatment
    Tension1st: paracetamol 500–1000 mg or ibuprofen 400–800 mg q4–6h PRN. 2nd: caffeine-combination analgesic. Chronic (>7–9 days/mo): amitriptyline 10 mg nocte, titrate to max 100 mg
    Migraine — mild/moderateNSAID (celecoxib 200 mg OD) or paracetamol; consider antiemetic + hydration
    Migraine — severeSumatriptan 25–100 mg PO, may repeat at 2h
    Migraine — ER rescueMetoclopramide 10–20 mg IV + diphenhydramine 10 mg IV, or sumatriptan 6 mg SC
    Migraine prophylaxis (≥4 days/mo)Beta-blocker, topiramate/valproate, or amitriptyline/SSRI
    Cluster — acuteSumatriptan 6 mg SC (repeat ≥1h later) + high-flow O₂; CI in CVD/uncontrolled HTN
    Cluster — prophylaxisVerapamil 80 mg TID (exclude bradycardia first)
  • Sumatriptan contraindicated in cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, uncontrolled HTN, pregnancy. Paracetamol preferred in pregnancy.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain red flags using SNOOP4; headache diary; trigger avoidance.
  • Refer: psychology/CBT (tension), neurology (cluster), rheumatology + ophthalmology (giant cell arteritis).

Stroke / TIA

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • SOCRATES for the deficit, plus: any previous attacks and action taken. Onset time is the single most important data point — it sets the thrombolysis window.
  • BE-FAST: Balance (sudden loss), Eyes (visual disturbance), Face (droop), Arms (weakness/numbness), Speech (slurred), Time.
  • TIA = same as stroke but resolves <24h. Amaurosis fugax = sudden painless transient monocular vision loss ('curtain coming down') — carotid embolic source.
🚩 Red flags
  • Sudden onset of symptoms · focal neurological deficit (hemiparesis, aphasia, hemianopia) · symptoms lasting more than a few minutes.
  • ABCD2 score stratifies 48-hour stroke risk after TIA and drives the admit-vs-outpatient decision. CHA₂DS₂-VASc for AF-related stroke risk. Both on MDCalc.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: BP, HR, RR, temp, height, weight, BMI, waist circumference.
  • Full neurological exam: power, tone, sensation, reflexes, cranial nerves, meningeal signs if suspected. Fundoscopy + visual acuity. Lymph node, ENT, CVS, MSK exam.
MimicDistinguishing feature
Seizure (Todd's paresis)Focal weakness after a seizure, confusion, tongue biting, incontinence
Migraine with auraGradual onset, visual aura/sensory disturbance, often followed by headache
Multiple sclerosisRelapsing-remitting course in a younger patient
Hypo/hyperglycemiaAltered consciousness, responds to glucose correction
Hypertensive encephalopathyConfusion, headache, visual change, markedly elevated BP
Carotid/vertebral dissectionHead/neck pain, Horner's syndrome, young patient
Functional neurological disorderInconsistent exam findings

🔬Investigations

  • Initial: non-contrast CT brain within 20 minutes (rule out hemorrhage — this is the single highest-yield answer in this station) · glucose (rule out hypoglycemia mimicking stroke) · ECG (AF/MI) · CBC, U&E, coagulation, troponin, lipids, HbA1c.
  • Additional: CTA/MRA (most accurate for vessel occlusion) · MRI (posterior circulation/small infarcts) · carotid Doppler · echo (embolic source).

💊Management

  • ⚠ At the PHC — what NOT to do
    • Recognize early with BE-FAST; stabilize ABCs; check glucose immediately.
    • Do NOT give aspirin or any oral medication.
    • Do NOT attempt to lower blood pressure.
    • Keep NPO. Head elevated 30° if conscious; lateral position if reduced consciousness.
    • Oxygen only if SpO₂ <94%; consider intubation if GCS ≤8.
    • Prepare immediate referral within 4.5 hours of onset to a stroke-ready hospital.
  • Secondary prevention (post-acute): aspirin immediately or within 24h of thrombolysis; chronic — aspirin+dipyridamole or clopidogrel. Anticoagulation after 48h for cardioembolic causes (including AF). High-intensity statin for all regardless of cholesterol (atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg). BP target <130/80 after recovery — not during the acute phase.
  • IschemicHemorrhagic
    BP in acute phasePermissive HTN before reperfusion; <185/110 after thrombolysisAggressive: target <140–160 systolic
    ReperfusionAlteplase ≤4.5h; thrombectomy ≤6–24h for LVONot applicable
    Key contraindications to thrombolysisICH on imaging, recent major surgery/trauma/GI bleed (21d), BP >185/110, platelets <100k, INR >1.7, glucose <50—

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain red flags/safety-net (sudden weakness, speech difficulty, facial droop — call EMS, don't wait).
  • Refer: neurology, physio/OT/speech therapy, cardiology (embolic source), endocrinology (uncontrolled diabetes).
  • Follow-up focused on functional recovery, recurrence prevention, medication adherence.

Depression

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport — this station rewards a gentle, non-judgemental opening more than any other.

•History

  • Onset and duration of depressed mood — ≥2 weeks is the diagnostic threshold. Ask about previous episodes.
  • SIG E CAPS — depressive symptoms (maps directly to PHQ-9): Sleep change · Interest loss · Guilt · Energy loss · Concentration difficulty · Appetite change ± weight change · Psychomotor retardation · Suicidal ideation/plan/attempts, and sexual (libido loss).
  • DIG FAST — screen for mania before starting an antidepressant: Distractibility · Indiscretion (excess pleasurable activity) · Grandiosity · Flight of ideas · Activity increase · Sleep deficit · Talkativeness.
  • GAD-7 domains if anxiety coexists: nervous/on edge, can't control worry, trouble relaxing, restless, easily irritated, worry excessively, feels something awful may happen.
  • Ask directly about delusions and hallucinations (psychotic features).
  • Postpartum: mood symptoms resolving within 2 weeks = baby blues. Persisting >2 weeks or impairing function = postpartum depression — actively screen.
🚩 Red flags — these change the station
  • Active suicidal ideation or plan — this is very likely to be directly tested; have a structured risk-assessment approach ready, not an improvised one.
  • Psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions).
  • Severe functional impairment — self-neglect, or neglect of dependent children.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: appearance (grooming), vital signs, BMI.
  • Screen for hypothyroidism and neurological deficits.
  • Mental Status Exam: mood ('sad, low' — in the patient's own words), affect (restricted), thought content (hopelessness, guilt, suicidal ideation), cognition (concentration).

🔬Investigations

  • Not required to diagnose depression. Order only to exclude organic mimics: CBC (anemia), electrolytes, TFTs (hypothyroidism), B12/folate if indicated, pregnancy test, urine toxicology.

💊Management

  • Non-pharm: CBT is first-line for mild–moderate depression. Exercise, sleep hygiene, social engagement, family involvement.
  • Pharm: 2nd-generation antidepressant (SSRI/SNRI) first-line. Acute phase 6–12 weeks to remission; continuation 4–9 months to prevent relapse; maintenance to prevent recurrence after ≥1 year.
  • Special situations: breastfeeding — sertraline or paroxetine preferred (fluoxetine's long half-life risks infant irritability/sedation, but can continue if she's already responded well to it, with monitoring). Antenatal — sertraline.
  • 🎓 High-yield OSCE detail — tapering strategy
    • 10% dose reduction per week to match serotonin transporter occupancy decline.
    • Or: reduce 25% every 4 weeks, or 12.5% every 2 weeks, over 3–4 months.
    • Cross-taper: decrease current drug while increasing the new one simultaneously.
    • Direct switch: stop current, start new immediately.
    • Moderate switch: taper, then 2–3 day washout, then start new drug conservatively.
    • Conservative switch: taper, then 7-day washout (most medications), then start conservatively.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain red flags (suicidal thoughts) and safety-net explicitly — don't skip this even if risk seems low.
  • Periodic health maintenance; if postpartum, screen at 4–6 weeks or the 2-month well-child visit using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.
  • Follow-up: 2–4 weeks after starting treatment, then monthly for 12 months. Continue medication ≥6–12 months after remission (longer if recurrent). Taper gradually if discontinuing.
  • Refer: psychiatry (suicidal risk, psychosis, treatment resistance), psychology (CBT), internal medicine/endocrinology (organic cause suspected).

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • TrimesterExpected physiological changes

  • Obstetric history: gravida/para/abortions/living children; LMP → EDD by Naegele's rule; prior pregnancies (miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm/post-term, delivery mode, complications).
  • Gynae: pre-conception menstrual pattern, infertility/PCOS history, prior contraception, last Pap/STI history.
  • Medical/surgical: chronic disease (DM, HTN, thyroid, renal, cardiac, epilepsy, asthma, autoimmune, IDA/SCA), DVT/PE or thrombophilia, uterine/cervical/pelvic surgery.
  • Medications — explicitly screen for teratogens: ACE inhibitors, valproate, retinoids, levothyroxine dosing. Supplements (folic acid, iron, calcium, vitamin D), immunizations, herbal/OTC use.
🚩 Red flags — urgent referral
  • Severe headache/visual change — pre-eclampsia/eclampsia.
  • BP ≥140/90 + proteinuria — hypertensive disorder of pregnancy.
  • Vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage — miscarriage, previa, abruption, ROM.
  • Severe abdominal pain — abruption, ectopic, labor complication.
  • Decreased fetal movement — fetal distress/demise.
  • Preterm contractions <37 weeks.
  • High fever/rigors, seizures/LOC, sudden facial/hand swelling, severe dyspnea/chest pain, heavy postpartum bleeding.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: pallor, jaundice, cyanosis, edema; BP at every visit; height, weight, BMI.
  • Pre-eclampsia signs: headache, visual blurring, RUQ tenderness, excessive swelling. Thyroid, varicose veins, DVT signs.
  • Obstetric exam (from ~12 weeks): abdominal size/shape, visible fetal movement (>20wk); fundal height; Leopold's maneuvers for lie/presentation (from 32wk); head engagement (from 36wk); fetal heart sounds from 18–20wk (normal 120–160 bpm). Pelvic exam only if indicated, not routine.

🔬Investigations

Well Baby Clinic

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, build rapport with the parent/caregiver — and engage the child directly if age-appropriate.

•History

  • Perinatal history — three phases: prenatal (maternal HTN/DM/infections/GDM/pre-eclampsia, exposures, pregnancy complications); natal (gestational age, delivery mode/presentation, birth weight, complications); postnatal (NICU admission + reason, jaundice, resuscitation, feeding, birth vaccinations).
  • Feeding: breast vs formula — frequency, difficulties, duration, wet diaper count; vitamin D supplementation; solids introduced (if >6 months).
  • Growth: weight/height/head circumference concerns, plotted on growth chart. Key numbers: birth weight doubles by 5mo, triples by 1y. Length +50% by 1y, doubles by 4y. Head circumference grows fastest in year one (~1 cm/month).
  • Developmental milestones by age (see table below). Immunization status against the national schedule + any adverse reactions.
  • Safety: car seat use, home safety. Screening: ADHD, autism.

🩺Physical Examination

  • General: alertness, parent interaction; growth chart (height, weight, head circumference) plotted against age norms.
  • Head (fontanelles, shape, sutures) · eyes (red reflex, tracking, squint) · ears (response to sound) · mouth (cleft, teeth, thrush) · chest (shape, breath sounds) · heart (murmurs, femoral pulses) · abdomen (distension, organomegaly, umbilicus) · genitalia (descended testes, hernia, discharge) · spine/limbs (tone, hip exam — Barlow/Ortolani if <6 months) · skin (rash, birthmarks, jaundice, cyanosis) · reflexes.

  • Not routine unless indicated: Hb/Hct at 9–12mo (esp. if preterm/low birth weight) · lead screening if risk factors · urinalysis if UTI suspected · TFTs if growth/developmental concerns · vitamin D if rickets/malnutrition/limited sun · G6PD if neonatal jaundice history · hip ultrasound 4–6wk for DDH if breech/female · celiac screen if growth faltering.

💊Management

  • 🎓 Safety counseling — commonly tested details
    • Rear-facing car seat until age 2, ideally until the seat's weight limit (30–36 kg).
    • Infant walkers discouraged — no developmental benefit, increase fall risk.
    • Water heater ≤49°C to prevent scald burns.
    • Sleep: supine on a firm mattress, no soft bedding — SIDS prevention. Breastfeeding, pacifier use, and room-sharing (without bed-sharing) are protective; parental smoking/substance use and bed-sharing increase risk.
    • Screen time: none <18 months (except video calls); limited educational content 18–24 months; ≤1 hour/day ages 2–5.
    • Feeding: breastfeed to ≥6 months, ideally 12. Delay cow's milk to 12 months. Introducing allergenic foods (peanut, egg) before age 1 reduces food allergy risk — don't delay them.
  • Vitamin D 400 IU daily for all children. Iron: exclusively breastfed term infant 4–12mo → 1 mg/kg/day; preterm 1–12mo → 2 mg/kg/day if breastfed; older child 3–6 mg/kg/day divided.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain red flags/safety-net.
  • Periodic health maintenance and routine visit schedule (2, 4, 6, 9, 12 months).
  • Refer to pediatrician: developmental delay in any domain, poor weight gain/faltering growth, dysmorphic features/abnormal tone, persistent vomiting/dehydration, heart murmur, hepatosplenomegaly, or parental concern about vision/hearing/behavior.

Acute Abdomen

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport — assess for distress/instability before proceeding.

•History

  • Site by diagnosis: epigastric (pancreatitis, PUD), RUQ (biliary), periumbilical→RIF migration (appendicitis), LLQ (diverticulitis), suprapubic (bladder/gynae), flank (renal).
  • Onset: sudden (torsion, perforation, obstruction) vs gradual (inflammation). Character: colicky (obstruction/renal colic) vs constant/boring (peritonitis, pancreatitis). Radiation: shoulder (diaphragmatic irritation/perforation), groin/testis (ureteric colic).
🚩 Red flags
  • Peritonitis (rigid abdomen, rebound) → urgent surgical review.
  • Septic physiology (fever, tachycardia, hypotension) → sepsis protocol.
  • Shock, uncontrolled bleeding, altered mental status.
  • Perforation — free air on imaging.
  • Obstructed/strangulated bowel (fever + localized peritonitis + lactic acidosis).
  • Charcot's triad (fever, jaundice, RUQ pain) ± Reynolds' pentad (+shock, +confusion) → cholangitis, urgent decompression.
  • Suspected ectopic pregnancy or ovarian torsion → gynecologic emergency.

🩺Physical Examination

  • Inspect (distension, scars, Cullen's/Grey-Turner's ecchymosis) → auscultate (high-pitched = obstruction, absent = late peritonitis) → percuss (tympany = obstruction, dullness = mass/ascites) → palpate (tenderness, guarding, rebound, organomegaly). PR exam if indicated; pelvic if female.

🔬Investigations

  • Initial: CBC, U&E, LFTs, amylase/lipase, lactate, CRP, coagulation profile, glucose (exclude DKA), blood cultures if septic, calcium (hypercalcemia mimics acute abdomen), urinalysis, β-hCG in every female of childbearing age, ABG if concerned for ischemia/sepsis, upright CXR/AXR (free air), ECG, type & crossmatch.
  • Additional: CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast is the gold standard for most etiologies. RUQ ultrasound first-line for gallbladder pathology (pregnancy-safe). CT KUB for renal colic. CT angiography for mesenteric ischemia. Pelvic ultrasound for gynae causes.

💊Management

  • General initial steps, every acute abdomen: ABCs, IV crystalloid if hypotensive, NPO, NG decompression if obstruction suspected, urinary catheter to monitor output, adequate analgesia — opioids are acceptable, do not withhold, antiemetics, empiric IV antibiotics if sepsis/peritonitis suspected, urgent surgical/gynae/medical consult.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain red flags/safety-net.
  • Periodic health maintenance.
  • Refer immediately (surgical): peritonitis, perforation, strangulation, appendicitis with peritonitis, obstructed/strangulated SBO, septic cholecystitis.
  • Follow-up: imaging/colonoscopy after diverticulitis resolves; outpatient surgical review after conservative management; metabolic stone workup if recurrent renal colic.

Red Eye

👋 Introduce yourself, confirm identity, and build rapport.

•History

  • One eye or both; sudden vs gradual; character of redness (diffuse, sectoral, ciliary flush) and discharge (watery, purulent, mucous); relation to contact lens use or trauma; worse with light (uveitis, keratitis) vs improved with artificial tears (dry eye).
  • Ask specifically about trauma, similar past episodes, and treatments already tried.
🚩 Red flags — the ones that separate 'reassure' from 'refer today'
  • Severe eye pain · decreased or blurred vision, sudden vision loss · photophobia.
  • Ciliary flush (redness concentrated around the cornea, not diffuse) — this single finding should raise your differential immediately.
  • Corneal opacity/ulcer · fixed mid-dilated pupil (angle-closure glaucoma) · proptosis or restricted eye movement (orbital cellulitis).
  • Systemic symptoms (fever, headache, vomiting) · trauma or recent ocular surgery.

🩺Physical Examination

  • Visual acuity first (Snellen/Rosenbaum) — decreased acuity flags a serious cause (keratitis, glaucoma).
  • External inspection (eyelid swelling/redness/discharge) → conjunctiva/sclera (redness pattern, discharge, foreign body — always evert the upper lid) → pupil reaction (swinging light test for RAPD → optic nerve involvement) → ocular movements (painful/restricted → orbital cellulitis).
  • Fundoscopy (dilate if needed) — hemorrhages, papilledema. Preauricular lymph nodes (enlarged in viral conjunctivitis).
  • Special tests: fluorescein stain (corneal ulcer/abrasion; dendritic ulcer pattern = HSV keratitis) · tonometry (raised IOP = glaucoma).

  • Usually clinical. If severe/red flag: corneal scraping + culture (suspected keratitis), CBC/CRP if systemic infection, orbital CT/MRI if orbital cellulitis. Autoimmune screen (ANA, RF) if recurrent uveitis/scleritis.

💊Management

  • Stop contact lens use; avoid rubbing; hand hygiene, avoid sharing towels.

    Allergic conjunctivitisTopical antihistamine (olopatadine BID) or mast-cell stabilizer; oral antihistamine
    Subconjunctival hemorrhageReassurance — resolves in 2–3 weeks
  • Do not patch the eye for simple conjunctivitis — this is a specifically tested point of common practice error.

✅Plan & Closing

  • Explain expected course (conjunctivitis resolves in 1–2 weeks) and explicit safety-net: return if no improvement in 2–3 days, or new pain/vision loss/photophobia/systemic symptoms.
  • Refer urgently/ER-ophthalmology: any red flag; keratitis (intensive topical antibiotics); uveitis (topical steroids + cycloplegics); scleritis (NSAIDs/immunosuppression); acute angle-closure glaucoma — emergency, IV acetazolamide + topical beta-blocker + laser iridotomy; orbital cellulitis — admission + IV antibiotics.
